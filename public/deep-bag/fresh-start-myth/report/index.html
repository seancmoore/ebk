Summary of findings

1. Selection dominates the naive story. Players who get traded were rising relative to peers two seasons earlier (+1.20 pts/gm, p=<0.0001) and falling the season before the trade (-1.07, p=0.0001). Teams sell after a disappointing year that followed a good one. A single before-season is a biased baseline; the primary specification uses a two-season pre-trade average.

2. The average trade is followed by underperformance versus matched peers (-1.30 pts/gm, boot95 [-1.90, -0.73], q<0.001, two-year baseline), but the size of that estimate is specification-dependent (−0.42 to −1.30 across specs) and part of it is the pre-trend the matching cannot fully remove (§3, §11).

3. Role is the one result robust to everything. Players acquired into a smaller role, measured by the acquiring team's Week-1 depth chart before any of that season's games, land -3.61 pts/gm below matched peers (p<0.0001, d≈−0.9). Intended promotions are roughly neutral. The effect holds in the early-career and prime stages (the small decline-stage cell, n=12, is null; see §5), in every offense-quality direction, at k=3/5/10, in the in-season cohort and on defense. As of §11b, under a ≥4-game floor and draft-round-pedigree matching applied directly to this same depth-chart measure (-3.53 to -3.55 pts/gm), and under cluster-resampled confidence intervals that account for shared control pools (§11b).

4. On both sides of the ball, trades move opportunity, not per-play quality. Defensive snap share falls 21 points for intended demotions while IDP-per-snap efficiency never moves; on offense, the earlier “WR gets more efficient per target” result does not survive trajectory matching.

5. Commitment predicts outcome. The negative average effect concentrates in low-commitment acquisitions: expiring contracts, low cap hits, and trades where the acquiring team signs no new deal. Players on large contracts or extended on arrival show no measurable effect. Note: this is not fully independent of finding 3: role, contract terms, and depth-chart placement are three observations of the same underlying front-office decision, made at the same time, so treat §9 as a second view of the role effect rather than a wholly separate confirmation of it.

Terms used interchangeably in this document and in the companion article: “Production Δ” (table columns here), “pts/gm” (this document's prose), and “fpts per game” (the public article) all refer to the same quantity: PPR fantasy points per game, matched-comparison difference-in-differences.

Of the 1,222 skill-position trades in the ledger, most are eliminated before they can be compared to anything: a player needs a usable stat line on both sides of the trade, and enough similar non-traded players to match against. The funnel below shows how many survive each requirement:

That drop from 1,222 to 243 raises an obvious worry: survivorship bias. If the players who got dropped mostly did so because they flopped after the trade, the 243 who remain would be a rosier sample than reality. The chart below tests that directly, breaking the 864 dropped events down by why they were dropped:

Figure 7. Of the 864 dropped events, 766 (89%) involve a player with no prior NFL stat season at all (draft-day and camp-body deals). Only 85 had a real prior season and then no season after the trade. The survivorship caveat is therefore mostly “never established”, not “traded and flopped”. Of dropped events with an after-season ≥ 2009, 25% appear on an injury report (Out/Doubtful/DNP) that season. Open item: for the 85, this pipeline can confirm they had real, established production before the trade and simply generate no after-trade row; it cannot currently tell apart the plausible reasons why (retirement, season-ending injury, roster cut before the after-window, or an after-season that falls outside our data horizon). We have not resolved which of these applies to this group, and until we do, they remain a genuine, unquantified edge case rather than evidence either for or against the main findings.

§2 Method

Trade. A dated front-office transaction in the nfldata ledger (not free agency or a waiver claim); one event per (player, season). Offseason trades (Jan–Aug): before = S−1, after = S. In-season (Sep–Dec): before = S−1, after = S+1, analyzed separately.

Controls. For each treated player: every same-position player in the same before-season who has a stat line in the after-season, stayed on one team across the window (and the prior season, for the two-year spec), has team-context data, and is within ±2 years of age. From that pool, the k=5 nearest by standardized Euclidean distance on (i) pre-trade production and (ii) change in team offensive efficiency (yards per play, computed from the panel; isolates offense from defense/special-teams scoring). Correction from v0.3: earlier passes described but did not implement the age caliper; it is now enforced.

Estimator. DiD = (after − pre) for the treated player minus the mean of the same for his matched controls. Three specifications: (a) single-year pre = season B; (b) trajectory-matched: controls also matched on season B−1; (c) primary: pre = average of B−1 and B. Outcomes: production (PPR pts/gm), opportunity (attempts / carries+targets / targets per game), efficiency (pts per opportunity). Inference: two-sided one-sample t-test, 2,000-resample bootstrap percentile CI, Cohen's d, Benjamini–Hochberg q over the family of primary tests (n≥10).

Placebos. The same matching applied to windows in which the player was not traded: (B−1 → B) and (B−2 → B−1).

Role. Two measures. Realized: rank 1/2/3+ by opportunity share among the team's same-position players (post-outcome, partly definitional for opportunity). Intended: depth_team rank on the acquiring team's first regular-season depth chart (Offense formation) vs. the same on the old team the prior year, set before any of the season's games are played. Depth-chart rows: 203,346 (offense skill, REG).

Defense. DL/LB/DB, 2012+, offseason trades, ≥4 games both years. Production = IDP points/gm (solo 1, assist 0.5, sack 2, INT 3, PD 1, FF 2, TD 6); opportunity = defensive snap share; efficiency = IDP per 100 snaps; team context = points allowed per game; intended role from the Defense-formation depth chart.

§3 Selection: the pre-trade trajectory

Before asking what a trade does to a player, it helps to know what kind of player gets traded in the first place. The chart below tracks production relative to matched peers across three one-year windows bracketing the trade: two placebo windows where no trade happened yet, then the real season right before the deal.

Figure 1. Production relative to matched peers in three consecutive one-year windows. The two left points are placebos (no trade occurred). Traded players outgained peers two years out and fell behind the year before the trade.

Placebo B−2→B−1+1.20 pts/gmn=205, p=<0.0001
Placebo B−1→B-1.07 pts/gmn=207, p=0.0001
Residual pre-trend after trajectory matching-1.24 pts/gmn=236, p=<0.0001

Matching controls on both prior seasons does not remove the pre-trend: stayers who dipped as hard as the soon-to-be-traded are rare (teams keep the dipping players they believe in), so the nearest available controls dipped less. This is a structural identification limit, not a coding choice. Consequences: (i) single-year DiD (-0.42) is biased toward zero if the dip mean-reverts and biased negative if it persists; (ii) the two-year baseline (-1.30) treats the dip as noise and is the more conservative-against-mean-reversion estimate; (iii) the trajectory-matched estimate (-0.62) sits between them. Results that are stable across all three (the role effects) are the ones to trust.

§11 Limitations & open items

  • Identification. Matching cannot fully reproduce the pre-trade dip (residual pre-trend -1.24, §3). The average effect of a trade is therefore bounded, not pinned: between roughly -1.3 (two-year baseline) and -0.4 (single-year) pts/gm depending on how much of the dip would have persisted. This is a different quantity from the 0.5–1.3 pts/gm spread shown in Figure 2, which is how far individual per-stratum estimates (by position, by stage) move when the specification changes; the -0.4-to-1.3 range here is the bound on the single overall estimate. Role-conditional effects are stable across these assumptions.
  • Intended role is still a proxy for the team's plan. Depth charts are club-published and inconsistently maintained for some franchises; coverage is 192 of 243 events.
  • Survivorship. Results describe trades where the player had a prior season and a subsequent one. The dropped set is mostly never-established players (§1), which narrows but does not eliminate the caveat.
  • Small cells. Decline-stage, TE, and several interaction cells have n<15, and this is not a purely cosmetic caveat: it is the direct reason several position-by-role cells fail the pooled BH correction in §4. Per outputs/tables/fdr_table.csv, RB×demoted (n=10, p=0.030) and RB×promoted (n=17, p=0.037) do not survive correction at q<0.05, and TE×promoted (n=9, §5) is small enough that it falls outside the tested family altogether. By contrast, the larger interaction cells, upgrade×demoted (n=36) and downgrade×demoted (n=25), do survive. Read the position-by-role breakdowns in §5 with that split in mind: the demotion pattern is well established where samples are large enough, and unresolved, not merely “small,” where they aren't.
  • Defense production metric. IDP scoring is a convention, not a measure of defensive value; snap share is the cleaner defensive outcome.
  • Offense production metric (added v0.5). PPR fantasy points is a scoring convention, not a direct measure of on-field value: it weights receptions independent of yardage, which mechanically favors high-target WRs and pass-catching RBs over between-the-tackles rushers. We use it because it is the most complete single per-game production number available across positions in this pipeline, not because it is scoring-format-neutral. A rerun on a non-PPR or EPA-based outcome is a natural follow-up, not yet done.
  • Causal language (added v0.5). This is a matched-comparison observational design, not a randomized or quasi-experimental one (no instrument, discontinuity, or exogenous assignment of role). “Predicts” and “is associated with” are the right verbs for what's shown here; readers should discount any place in this document or the companion article that slips into “causes”/“drives”/“settles” language accordingly.
  • Accolades not sourced. No Pro Bowl / All-Pro / MVP-vote dataset is joined; team win/playoff context only (v0.3 §7b).
  • Three unaddressed confounds (added v0.7, honestly open, not ruled out). (1) Era/scoring environment: fpts is computed under a single PPR scoring rule applied retroactively across 2002–2025, a window that saw multiple rule changes affecting passing and receiving volume; no era adjustment is applied. (2) Injury status at the time of trade: this pipeline does not condition on whether a player was already injured when demoted, so a role loss driven by an undisclosed or unlisted injury would look identical, in these tables, to a role loss driven by fit. This can reverse the implied direction of the story for some cases. (3) Contract-year incentives: players entering a walk year may play through injury, or conversely be benched, for reasons tied to their own contract situation rather than to how they fit their new team. None of these three has been checked against the data and none should be read as ruled out; they are named here as open items, not as caveats that have been addressed.
  • Legacy scripts. Scripts 04–10 are the v0.2/v0.3 passes and are superseded by 11–18; kept for provenance.

§11b v0.6 robustness checks (added, run alongside originals)

Two additional robustness checks were run against the primary two-year-baseline specification (script 13_two_year_baseline_v2.py). Outputs are in outputs/tables/*_v2.csv; the original script and its outputs are untouched, and these are supplementary, not a replacement.

  • Minimum-games floor. The primary spec had no floor on games played in the before/after seasons used to compute per-game rates, unlike the defense spec (§8, which requires ≥4 games both years). Adding the same ≥4-game floor to the offense spec drops the main cohort from n=243 to n=187 (71 treated events had a sub-4-game season somewhere in the window). Headline numbers barely move: overall -1.284 (was -1.300), role:demoted -3.128 (was -3.537), role:promoted +0.120 (was +0.204).
  • Draft-round matching. Player's draft round was added as a third matching dimension (alongside pre-trade production and team-efficiency change), to check whether the demotion effect is partly a pedigree effect in disguise. On the games-floored n=187 cohort: overall -1.292, role:demoted -2.974, role:promoted +0.184. Both effects hold, within about half a point of the two-dimension match. (These are raw, uncorrected p-values; see the multiple-comparison note directly below, which has not confirmed a separate BH correction for this games-floored re-run.)
  • Multiple-comparison correction on the games-floor re-run (honest gap). The games-floored (n=187) and draft-round-matched estimates above are reported as raw means and p-values; we cannot confirm from the pipeline outputs that these were run back through a Benjamini–Hochberg correction of their own, separate from the pooled 50-comparison family in §4 that was built on the uncorrected n=243 cohort. Treat the n=187 numbers as not separately corrected for multiple comparisons until that is run and confirmed, rather than assuming the §4 correction covers them.

Neither check moved the headline findings enough to change the conclusions in §4–§5. Note on the correction itself, corrected here from an earlier draft of this section: outputs/tables/fdr_table.csv shows the BH correction is run across one pooled family of 50 comparisons, with simple strata (overall, position, stage, role, direction) and interaction strata (e.g. dir:lateral|role:demoted, role:demoted|stage:prime) ranked together, not split into a separate primary family and an exploratory interaction-cell family. Within that single pooled ranking, the core results hold up: overall (q<0.001) and role:demoted (q<0.001) sit at the top of the ranked list, while several interaction cells and small position-by-role cells (see §11) fall well down it and fail to survive at q<0.05. Full per-stratum q-values are in outputs/tables/fdr_table.csv and, for the games-floor cohort, outputs/tables/results_two_year_baseline_v2.csv; the latter's p-values were not separately re-corrected for multiple comparisons, a caveat repeated in the note below.

Which role measure was actually re-tested. The two checks above were first run through the realized (post-season opportunity-rank) role measure, not the intended (Week-1 depth chart) measure this section's headline number (§5, -3.61 pts/gm) actually quotes, an omission caught on the next review pass and closed here rather than left as a caveat. Re-running both checks through the depth-chart pipeline directly (script 14_depth_charts_v2.py, joined to the games-floor/draft-matched cohort) gives: intended-role demoted n=43 (was 57 pre-floor), mean -3.532 pts/gm, p<0.0001 with the games floor alone; -3.546 with draft-round-matched controls. Both land within 0.08 pts/gm of the original -3.607, so the headline number now has a direct, not borrowed, robustness check behind it. Full output in outputs/tables/results_intended_role_v2.csv.

Cluster-robust bootstrap check. The primary bootstrap CIs (§2) resample individual treated players, but their matched-control pools overlap across treated players sharing the same (position, season) block, which understates the true sampling variability. Re-running the bootstrap with resampling clustered at the (position, season) block instead of the individual player widens the realized-role-demoted 95% CI by about 22%. The point estimate is essentially unchanged and the widened interval still does not cross zero, so the finding holds. Readers should treat the uncorrected CIs reported elsewhere in this document, and in the companion article, as mildly optimistic about precision, not about the direction or size of the effect.

Clustered standard errors. The bootstrap CIs reported throughout this document (including the numbers just above) resample individual treated-player DiD values as independent draws. They likely aren't: within a (position, before-season) block, multiple treated players can draw overlapping k=5 control pools, which induces correlation the naive bootstrap doesn't see, and the bootstrap also never re-draws the control-side mean itself, so it understates the extra variance introduced by matching on only 5 neighbors. A cluster-bootstrap (script 21_cluster_bootstrap.py, resampling whole position×season clusters with replacement, 2,000 draws) run against the same headline strata found the CIs widen modestly and unevenly: realized-role demoted widens about 22% (95% CI [-4.33, -1.56] vs. the naive [-4.27, -1.99]), overall widens about 3%, intended/depth-chart demoted is essentially unchanged (about 9% narrower, within simulation noise). None of the widened intervals cross zero. (The "naive" baseline here is the normal-approximation CI, mean ± 1.96·SE, not the percentile bootstrap ("boot95") reported elsewhere in this document. The two are nearly identical for these strata, so the widening estimate would not materially change if compared to the percentile bootstrap instead, but they are not literally the same baseline.) This is a real, previously unaddressed source of understated uncertainty; the point estimates and directional conclusions survive it, but readers should treat the printed CIs elsewhere in this report as a lower bound on the true uncertainty, not the final word. Full output in outputs/tables/cluster_bootstrap_v2.csv.

Open items not resolved this pass. Two things this section does not fix, flagged honestly rather than papered over: (1) the draft-round matching dimension treats round number as a cardinal distance (round 1↔2 counted as equally "close" as round 6↔7, UDFA placed exactly one step past round 7): a modeling convenience, not a validated assumption; a coarser categorical bucketing (1st / 2nd–3rd / 4th–7th / UDFA) would be a cheap way to check sensitivity to this choice and has not been run.

(2) No dataset here captures a team's private read on a player (medical concerns, locker-room fit, a scheme-specific weakness that doesn't show up in aggregate stats), and such private information could drive both the demotion and the post-trade underperformance independent of role itself, which no amount of matching on public statistics can rule out. A concrete test that would bear on this and hasn't been run: match additionally on the pre-trade trend (the slope across B−2→B−1→B, not just the two-season level). If the demotion effect survives trend-matching as well as level-matching, that's meaningfully stronger evidence against the private-information story, since it would mean demoted players weren't already on a steeper private-information-correlated decline than their statistically similar peers. This is the single most valuable remaining robustness check and is left as the next item, not attempted here.

The Fresh-Start Myth

We matched 243 NFL trades against similar players who stayed put. The new city barely registered. Players handed a smaller job fell about 3.5 fantasy points a game behind, under every method we tried.
