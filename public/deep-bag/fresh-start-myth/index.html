The Fresh-Start Myth

We matched 243 NFL trades against similar players who stayed put. The new city barely registered. Players handed a smaller job fell about 3.5 fantasy points a game behind, under every method we tried.

Kenny Pickett was the only quarterback taken in the first round of the 2022 draft. He played 13 games for Pittsburgh as a rookie and 12 the next year, and on March 15, 2024, it traded him to Philadelphia. The Eagles’ Week 1 depth chart that September listed him second, behind Jalen Hurts, which is about where anyone would have guessed. He threw 42 passes all season.

Within a year and a half he was traded twice more, to Cleveland in March 2025 and to Las Vegas that August, and every move came with the same two words attached: fresh start.

His Philadelphia numbers look like a collapse. Pickett averaged 10.4 fantasy points a game across his two Pittsburgh seasons (PPR scoring, “fpts” from here on, used as a stand-in for total offensive output) and 5.0 with the Eagles. A raw before-and-after is the wrong yardstick, though, because plenty of quarterbacks who never changed teams also had a worse 2024 than 2023. So for every traded player we found the five players who looked most like him going in and stayed put: same position, same season, within two years of his age, closest in recent production and in how much their team’s offense changed. Pickett’s five were Jordan Love, Justin Herbert, Jarrett Stidham, Kyler Murray and Davis Mills. They fell by 3.1 fpts a game on average. Pickett fell by 5.3.

Matched peers

For each traded player, the five non-traded players at his position in the same season, within two years of his age, closest to him in production over the previous two years and in the change in their team’s offense. Everything in this piece is a traded player measured against his five.

The difference, about 2.2, is the unit this study counts in. We worked it out for 243 offensive trades from 2002 to 2025, taken from a ledger of dated front-office transactions so that free-agent signings and waiver claims stay out, and for 114 defensive trades from 2014 to 2025. Players who arrived to the same job or a bigger one ended up close to their matched peers. Players who arrived to a smaller one fell about three and a half fpts a game behind, and every version of the comparison put that gap somewhere between 2.9 and 3.6.

Nobody assigns NFL roles at random, so none of this is an experiment. These are patterns that predict how a traded player’s season goes, and the last section lists the ways they could still be wrong.

Sold after the slump

The first thing the data showed was who gets traded, and it undercuts most before-and-after takes you’ll read about a deal. The chart below runs the same matched comparison on the two seasons before each trade. The player hadn’t moved yet in either one, so a working method should find nothing there. It finds a rise and then a fall.

Teams sell after the dip. A player who rose and then sagged is the one a front office has started to doubt, and he would probably have looked better the next year whether he moved or not, because bad seasons tend to be followed by less bad ones. Measure a trade against the single season before it and the trade gets credit for a bounce that was coming anyway. The main numbers here use the average of the two seasons before the trade as the baseline, and every important result was rerun with the other ways of handling the dip. That choice swings the overall trade effect a lot. It barely touches the role effect.

What the depth chart knew

Every NFL team publishes a depth chart before Week 1. For a traded player it is the closest thing to the acquiring team’s plan, written down before he has played a snap for them. We compared each player’s spot on his new team’s first regular-season depth chart with his spot on his old team’s a year earlier and sorted every trade into three groups: acquired into a bigger job, the same job or a smaller one. Separately, the same trades were sorted by who actually got the touches once the season was played.

−3.5

fpts per game behind matched peers for players traded into a smaller job (73 trades, two-season baseline, sorted by touches). The bigger-job and same-job groups landed within a point of their peers.

One number from one method is easy to argue with, so the grid below holds every version that was run. Five ways of building the comparison go left to right, from the simplest (one season before the trade as the baseline) to the strictest (the Week 1 depth chart, a four-game minimum, and draft round added to the matching). The three kinds of role change run back to front, and the smaller-job row is the one nearest you. All five of its bars hang well below the glass.

Across the five methods the smaller-job gap runs from −2.9 to −3.6 fpts a game. No other group of real size comes close; the next biggest among the main splits by position, age and role is quarterbacks as a whole, at −2.0. The bigger-job row at the back is the one that wobbles, anywhere from +0.1 to +1.5 depending on the method, and only one of the five (matching on two separate prior seasons) gives it an interval that clears zero.

The demotion gap shows up wherever there are enough trades to test it: for early-career players (−4.4) and prime-age ones (−3.9), whether the new offense was better than the old one (−3.1) or worse (−3.5), and in midseason trades (−3.1) as well as offseason ones. It fades in two places. Players traded late in their careers show no clear demotion effect, though there are only 12 of them. And it isn’t established for the thinnest slices, like demoted running backs (10 trades) or tight ends (8) taken on their own. Both point the same direction, but neither clears the bar once you account for how many comparisons were run.

When the plan falls through

The Week 1 depth chart and the season’s actual touches put a player in the same group only 52% of the time. If the two sorts had nothing to do with each other they would agree about 38% of the time, so the plan carries real information and is still wrong a lot. The second grid crosses the two: the depth-chart plan runs left to right, what actually happened runs back to front, and the deepest bar sits where a planned demotion was carried out.

Those kept demotions are 33 players whose new team planned to use them less, and did. They finished 4.6 fpts a game behind their peers. Planned demotions that only partly happened still trailed, by 2.6 for the 17 who kept the same share of touches and by 1.6 for the 7 who ended up getting more. The promotion side is only a lead. The 11 planned promotions that came true averaged 2.9 ahead of their peers and the 11 that fell through averaged 1.5 behind, but with 11 players apiece neither number can be told apart from zero.

One caution about this grid. Touches and fantasy points move together almost by definition, so the “what happened” axis flatters itself. The depth chart is set before the season starts, which makes it the cleaner of the two measures, and on demotion it gives the same answer.

Denver supplied both halves of the story two years apart. In March 2010 the Broncos sent Peyton Hillis, a seventh-round pick who had opened the previous season third on their depth chart, to Cleveland in the deal for Brady Quinn. Cleveland listed him second. He ran for 1,177 yards and 11 touchdowns that year, finished 8.7 fpts a game ahead of his matched peers, and was voted onto the cover of Madden NFL 12. In March 2012 Denver traded Tim Tebow to the Jets after signing Peyton Manning, and New York’s depth chart also moved him up, from third to second. He threw eight passes that season and finished 12.9 behind his peers, one of whom was Mark Sanchez, the starter he never displaced. Tebow is one of the three trades in the ringed cell.

On defense, count the snaps

Darron Lee is the cleanest single case in the data. The Jets drafted him 20th overall in 2016, and in 2018 he was on the field for 97% of their defensive snaps in the games he played. In May 2019 they traded him to Kansas City for a sixth-round pick. The Chiefs used him on 13% of theirs, 160 snaps all season.

Individual defensive production is hard to pin down (there is no defensive version of a target), so the defensive side of the study leans on the plainest number there is: snap share, the percentage of a defense’s plays a player is on the field for. Across the 114 defensive trades the offensive story repeats with less noise. The chart below sets two measures side by side for each kind of role change. On the left, snap share against matched peers swings with the role. On the right, production per 100 snaps hardly moves.

Players acquired into a smaller role lost about 21 points of snap share against their peers. Production per snap barely moved for anyone; no group showed a change that could be told apart from zero. A traded defender makes about as many plays per snap as he did before the trade, and his new team decides how many snaps he gets. Lee is an extreme case of both. On his 160 snaps in Kansas City he produced more per snap, relative to his matched peers, than he had in New York.

The contract said so first

If the depth chart is the plan, the contract is the plan with money behind it. Each trade was joined to the player’s contract at the time (150 of the 243 matched to one), and for all 243 we checked whether the acquiring team signed him to a new deal in the trade year. The chart below splits the trades by contract situation, with the low-commitment groups in the accent color.

Nearly all of the average trade’s negative effect sits in the low-commitment deals. Players with a year or less left on their contract finished 1.4 fpts a game behind their peers; those with two or more years left were close to even (−0.4, inside the noise). The bottom two-thirds of cap hits trailed by 1.4 to 1.6, and the top third, players taking up a median 9.6% of the cap, finished level. The 53 players their new team signed on arrival came out at −0.5, well inside the noise, against −1.5 for the 190 who got nothing. The contract and the depth chart record the same front-office decision at the same moment, and the technical report treats the two as one result seen twice.

The 2007 Patriots sit at the top of that range. They sent a second- and a seventh-round pick to Miami for Wes Welker and signed him to a new deal on arrival; he caught 112 passes and finished 9.2 fpts a game ahead of his peers. Eight weeks later a fourth-rounder brought Randy Moss from Oakland, again with a new deal on arrival, and Moss caught 23 touchdown passes, finishing 12.8 ahead. Across all 53, though, players signed on arrival finished roughly level with their peers. The floor looks like Matt Schaub in 2014: traded from Houston to Oakland for a sixth, on a reworked deal worth about 5% of the cap after taking up 13% in Houston, and finished the season with 10 pass attempts.

Young, and often a quarterback

Split by age and the damage concentrates on players traded before or during their primes: 1.8 fpts a game behind their peers for early-career players and 1.4 for prime-age ones, against almost nothing (−0.2) for players traded on the far side of it. A young player who gets traded is often one his old team has given up on, and draft pedigree doesn’t protect him. Traded first-round picks finished 1.6 behind their peers, as far back as any draft tier.

Quarterbacks look like the position trades ruin, and mostly that’s the same effect. Early-career quarterbacks finished 2.4 behind their peers. Prime-age ones trailed by 1.5, a gap that can’t be distinguished from zero (p = 0.14). A traded young quarterback is often a benched one with a new address, which is a fair description of a 25-year-old Pickett arriving in Philadelphia to back up Hurts.

The finding we threw out

An earlier version of this study had a result we liked a lot. Traded wide receivers seemed to get fewer targets but more out of each one, a fit-over-volume story with DeSean Jackson’s 2019 return to Philadelphia as its poster. It held up in the first version of the numbers. Once players were matched on two prior seasons instead of one, the per-target gain shrank to nothing, and Jackson’s 2019 turned out to be two games long in the data. The role effect went through the same change and held, so it carries this piece and the receiver story is down to this paragraph.

Running backs got the same treatment. Traded backs as a group did no better than their peers (−0.1). Traded backs handed a bigger job did better, about 1.8 fpts a game ahead, but there are 17 of them and the edge doesn’t survive the correction for running many comparisons at once. Trung Canidate is the type. A 2000 first-round pick who carried 17 times for the Rams in 2002, he was traded to Washington the following February, got the starting job, ran for 597 yards, and was replaced a year later when Washington traded for Clinton Portis.

What could still be wrong

The role result is the one to lean on. It comes from a real trade ledger, real birth dates, Week 1 depth charts, snap counts and contracts, and a matched comparison that was run several ways and tested on seasons when no trade happened. It held when each player had to have at least four games in each season, so a handful of short seasons couldn’t drive it, and when draft round was added to the matching, so pedigree couldn’t quietly stand in for role. Under those checks demoted players still trailed their peers by about 3.0 to 3.5 fpts a game. The confidence intervals were also redone the stricter way, resampling whole position-and-season groups because traded players in the same season can share comparison players. That widened the interval for demotion measured by touches by about 22% and the overall trade effect by about 3%, left the depth-chart demotion interval essentially unchanged, and none of the demotion intervals came near zero.

The overall “trades hurt” number is real but soft. Peers finished ahead by somewhere between 0.4 and 1.3 fpts a game depending on how the pre-trade slump is handled, and that range comes from a limit of the design. Stayers who dipped exactly as hard as the soon-to-be-traded players are rare, because teams keep the dipping players they still believe in, so the best available comparisons dipped a little less. Even matched on both prior seasons, the traded players were still trending 1.24 fpts a game worse beforehand. That leftover trend is why this piece leans on the role effect instead.

Then there is who’s missing. Of the 1,222 skill-position trades in the ledger, 243 had what this design needs: two prior seasons, a season after, and enough similar players to match against. The obvious worry is that the dropped trades are the ones that flopped. Most of them never had a career to flop from. Of the 864 that fell out at the first cut, 766 involved players with no NFL stat season before the trade at all, mostly draft-day swaps and camp bodies. Only 85 were established players who simply have no season after the trade, and there is no telling yet how many of those were retirements, injuries, roster cuts or a data window that ends too soon.

The worst trade in the sample

Percy Harvin, 2013: Minnesota’s top receiver, third on Seattle’s opening depth chart, one regular-season game played. −19.1 fpts a game against his peers. It is also exactly the kind of case an injury can explain.

Three things this study hasn’t checked and doesn’t claim to have ruled out. It doesn’t adjust for how the league changed across 24 seasons; rule changes and the drift toward passing change what a typical fpts total looks like, though every comparison here is made within a single season. It doesn’t know a player’s injury status at the moment of the trade, and a demotion can be a team quietly benching a player it already knew was hurt, which would change the story for those players. And a player entering a walk year has his own reasons, unrelated to his new team, to play through pain or to be benched.

For Pickett, the record now runs to three trades in under a year and a half. The first is the only one this study can grade with a depth chart on both ends, and it graded out a little better than the typical planned demotion: 2.2 fpts a game behind the five quarterbacks who stayed home, against 3.6 for the average player whose new depth chart listed him lower than his old one had. His line for the Eagles that year was five games and 42 passes.
