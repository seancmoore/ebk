The Fresh-Start
Myth

We tracked two decades of NFL trades against players who stayed put. A change of scenery mostly isn't associated with a different player. What predicts the outcome is the job waiting on the other end.

A Deep Bag investigation · 243 trades analyzed, of 1,222 reviewed · 2002–2025

In March 2010, the Denver Broncos traded a backup running back named Peyton Hillis to Cleveland for Brady Quinn, a change-of-scenery deal nobody outside Ohio noticed. Hillis had opened the previous season third on Denver's depth chart. Cleveland listed him second, and by October he was the only back they had. By the end of that season he had 1,177 rushing yards and enough name recognition to beat Michael Vick in a nationwide vote for the cover of Madden NFL 12.

Two years later, in the same month, the Broncos made another trade that was supposed to write the same story. Tim Tebow, a national phenomenon who had just won Denver a playoff game with one perfect throw, was shipped to the Jets after the team signed Peyton Manning. New York's Week 1 depth chart listed him as the No. 2 quarterback. On paper, a fresh start. In practice he threw 8 passes all season, because the Jets were never going to bench Mark Sanchez for him and everyone in the building knew it.

Same team of origin. Same “fresh start” press release. One is a folk legend and one is a punchline, and the usual explanation (Hillis wanted it more, Tebow couldn't throw) is the kind of thing you say when you haven't checked.

So we checked. We took every skill-position trade since 2002 that we could verify as a real, dated, front-office transaction, not “he changed teams,” which would sweep in free agency. Then we compared each traded player against five near-identical players who weren't traded, what we'll call his matched peers throughout this piece: players who look statistically identical to him going into the trade, same position, same season, within two years of age, same recent production, except they weren't traded. We measured that production in PPR fantasy points per game, or fpts (standard points-per-reception scoring), our stand-in for overall offensive output.

The only question worth asking is not “did he get better?” but “did he do better than he would have anyway?” We ran that comparison three different ways, on offense and defense, with real depth charts, real snap counts and real contracts.

One thing to be clear about before any of the findings: this is a study of who actually got which job after a trade, not a controlled experiment. No one randomly assigned some players to get traded and others to stay put, so everything below is a pattern the data predicts, not a lever we watched anyone pull. We'll use words like “predicts” and “is associated with” on purpose, and you should read the piece the same way.

01 Teams sell after the dip, not before it

The first thing the data says has nothing to do with what happens after a trade. It's about who gets traded. Two seasons before the deal, the players in our sample improved by about 1.2 fpts a game more than their matched peers did. The season right before the deal, they fell off by about 1.1 fpts a game more than their peers. Up, then down, then traded.

The chart below plots those two pre-trade windows on one shared scale, each measured against the same kind of matched peers, with the trade itself marked at the right. The small p-values under each point are a statistical way of saying how likely it is that a gap this size is just random noise; the lower the number, the more confidence we have that it isn't.

That is not a story about trades. It's a story about front offices: they don't sell the player they've soured on at the top of his value, they sell him the year after a disappointment. Which means the naive way to study this, comparing last season to this season, is rigged from the start, because a player coming off an unusually bad year will look like he “bounced back” whether he was traded or not. Everything below uses the average of the two seasons before the trade as the baseline instead, and we flag which results hold up when the method changes and which don't.

02 It was never the trade. It was the job.

Here is the one result that survived every version of the analysis we could throw at it. We looked at the acquiring team's first regular-season depth chart, the club's own published plan for the player, set before a single snap was played, and compared it to where he'd been listed on his old team the year before. Some players were acquired into a bigger job. Some into the same one. Some into a smaller one.

The chart below shows how each of those three groups did against its matched peers, sorted two ways: once by the plan on the depth chart, and once by who actually got the touches. There's a lot going on in it at once, so here's what to look for: two bars per group, one for the plan and one for what actually happened, and any bar with diagonal hatching and a dashed edge means that number can't be told apart from zero, which is a different thing from it being small.

Players acquired into a smaller role finished the season about 3.5 fpts per game behind comparable players who stayed put. We ran the comparison three different ways (we call them specifications), and the gap ranged from about 2.9 to 3.5. It never came close to zero.

That is the largest gap in the study for any big group of players. The next biggest, quarterbacks as a whole, is about 2.0. It shows up clearly at the positions and splits with enough trades to test: on offense and on defense, for early-career and prime-age players, whether the new offense was better or worse than the old one, and in midseason deals as well as offseason ones. It is not established for the smallest slices, such as demoted running backs or tight ends taken alone (8 to 10 players each). The direction is the same there, but those samples are too thin to clear our bar once you account for how many comparisons we ran. For contrast, most position and age splits move by 0.5 to 1.3 fpts a game depending on the specification.

Players acquired into a bigger role did, on average, roughly what their matched peers did. Not worse. Not dramatically better.

Crossing the plan against what actually happened adds detail, with a warning about size. Of the 25 players whose new depth chart promised a bigger job, 11 actually got the touches, and they averaged nearly 3 fpts a game ahead of their peers. Another 11 never got the extra work and averaged about 1.5 behind. That is 11 players apiece, too few to be sure of either number, so read it as a lead, not a finding.

The demotion side is firmer. The 33 planned demotions that played out averaged about 4.6 fpts behind their peers, the worst group on the board, and planned demotions that didn't fully play out still trailed. One caution: touches and fantasy points move together almost by definition, which is why we lean on the depth chart, set before the season, as the cleaner measure.

Four trades from the edges of the sample show how wide that range runs.

Percy Harvin, 2013Minnesota's WR1 → third on Seattle's opening depth chart, hurt most of the year. −19.1 fpts per game vs. peers, the worst outcome in the sample. Darron Lee, 2019Former first-round Jets linebacker, traded to Kansas City for a sixth. Went from 97% of the Jets' defensive snaps to 13% of the Chiefs'. 160 defensive snaps all year. Peyton Hillis, 2010Third back in Denver → second in Cleveland → the starter. +8.7 fpts per game vs. peers. Denzel Perryman, 2021Signed by Carolina in the offseason, dealt to the Raiders in late August for a sixth. Snap share went from 40% to 83%; more than 140 tackles and a Pro Bowl.

These four are the tails of the distribution, not the middle of it: picked to show the range, not the typical case. The typical demoted player lands closer to −3.5 than to Harvin's −19.1; the typical promoted one is closer to flat than to Hillis's +8.7. Keep that in mind for everything below.

Run the two Broncos trades back through this. Hillis: a promotion the team meant and delivered. Tebow: a promotion on paper (QB3 in Denver's opening 2011 chart, QB2 in New York's) that the Jets had no intention of honoring. The depth chart is the plan, and it predicts the outcome about as well as anything in this study predicts anything: once you know what job the player was acquired to do, none of the surrounding scenery adds much on top of it.

The trade doesn't predict what a player will do next. The job waiting for him on the other side does.

That job-over-city claim is the load-bearing finding of this piece, and it deserves the same scrutiny we just aimed at everything else, so three things are worth naming plainly before we go further. First, the overall “trades hurt” number is real but soft: it moves from about 0.4 to 1.3 fpts a game depending on exactly how you handle the fact that teams trade players who are already trending down, which is the pattern we found in section 01. That's a different number from the 0.5-to-1.3 range you'll see attached to individual groups elsewhere in this piece: this one is how far the single overall estimate can move; the other is how far each group's own estimate moves when you change the method on that group specifically. That range isn't just us picking different methods to see what sticks. It's a structural limit of the design: our matching can't fully undo that pre-trade dip, because stayers who dipped exactly as hard as the traded players are rare (teams tend to keep the players they still believe in), so the best available non-traded comparisons dipped a little less. No amount of re-slicing the data removes that; it's baked into what a matched comparison can and can't do.

Second, an earlier pass at this analysis turned up a result we liked and then watched fall apart under a more careful re-check. We killed it rather than keep it, and we walk through what happened in section 06, because it's the reason to trust the finding that replaced it.

Third, there are a few things we have not checked and are not claiming to have ruled out; more on those below, in “How sure are we.” Here's what held up.

03 Defense confirms the opportunity story, not the production story

Defensive players are harder to measure (there's no equivalent of “targets”), so we used the one thing that is unambiguous: snap share, meaning the percentage of the defense's plays a player is actually on the field for. Across 114 defensive trades since 2012, the pattern is the offensive one with the noise stripped out. Players acquired into a smaller role lost about 21 percentage points of snap share relative to peers. Players acquired into a bigger role gained about 12, though that estimate rests on only 10 trades and is too noisy to call settled.

The Darron Lee trade from section 02's ledger is worth revisiting here, because the defensive numbers are what explain it. A first-round linebacker who'd been a near-every-down player in New York was, on paper, still a linebacker in Kansas City. The snap chart says otherwise: his role collapsed by more than 80 percentage points the moment he changed teams. Nothing about his tackling ability was tested by that trade. His job was.

Here is the cleanest test we have of whether a new team “unlocks” a player: production per snap did not move. Not for the demoted, not for the promoted, not at any position: no group showed a change we could tell apart from zero. The overall change in raw production per game is noisier on defense than on offense, and for two of three position groups (DB, LB) it isn’t statistically significant by itself. A defender simply doesn’t rack up individual stats the way a receiver racks up targets, so game-to-game production is a blunter instrument here. What's clearer is the mechanism: a traded defender makes about the same number of plays per snap he did before. The team just decides how many snaps he gets, and that decision is what the numbers track cleanly, the same story as Lee's 97% turning into 13% with no change in what he could do on the field.

04 Follow the money, because the team already did

If the acquiring team's plan is what matters, you'd expect the acquiring team's money to tell you the plan. It does. We joined every trade to the player's contract at the time. The negative average effect of a trade lives almost entirely in the low-commitment ones. Players on expiring deals finished about 1.4 fpts per game behind peers. So did players outside the top third of cap hits (a cap hit is the share of the team's salary budget tied up in one player): the bottom two-thirds trailed by about 1.4 to 1.6, while the top third, players taking up around 10% of the cap, finished even. Those two cuts cover the 150 trades we could match to a contract.

The clearest split is whether the new team signed the player to anything on arrival. The 190 of 243 who got no new deal finished 1.5 fpts per game behind peers. The 53 who got a new deal from the acquiring team in the trade year finished statistically even with theirs (about 0.45 fpts per game behind, well inside the noise). This isn't a separate discovery from the role finding. The contract, the depth chart and the playing time are three views of the same front-office decision.

Here's what that looked like for three players on the extremes of the sample.

Wes Welker, 2007A restricted free agent in Miami. New England traded a second- and seventh-round pick for him and signed him to a new deal the same week. 112 catches. +9.2 fpts per game vs. peers. Randy Moss, 2007Oakland wanted to move him; New England paid a fourth-rounder and restructured his deal on arrival. 23 touchdowns, still the single-season record. +12.8 fpts per game vs. peers. Matt Schaub, 2014Benched in Houston, traded to Oakland for a sixth. His new deal there took up about 5% of the cap, down from about 13% in Houston. Ten pass attempts all season, released in March.

Welker and Moss are the ceiling of the “new deal” group, not its average, and Schaub is a reminder that a reworked contract isn't the same as a real commitment. Across the whole group, players extended on arrival finish roughly even with their peers, not nine or twelve fpts ahead. None of this means an extension makes a player better, either. It means the extension is the team telling you, in a language that's harder to spin than a press release, what job it acquired him for.

05 The young get hurt worst, and quarterbacks most of all

Split the sample by age and the damage concentrates on players traded before or during their prime: about 1.75 fpts per game behind peers for early-career players and about 1.4 for prime-age ones, versus essentially nothing for players traded on the far side of it. That's the same story again, seen from a different angle. A young player who gets traded is disproportionately a player his old team has given up on, and draft pedigree doesn't rescue him: traded first-round picks finished about 1.6 fpts per game behind their peers, as far back as any draft tier.

Quarterbacks look like the position trades ruin, and it's mostly this effect. Early-career QBs traded away finished 2.4 fpts per game behind peers. Prime-age QBs trailed by about 1.5, a gap too noisy to tell apart from zero. The data doesn't point to a new system breaking quarterbacks. It points to “traded young quarterback” often being a polite term for a benching that crossed state lines. Sam Howell played all 17 games for Washington at 23, was dealt to Seattle to back up Geno Smith, and threw 14 passes there the next season.

06 What we got wrong the first time

An earlier draft of this piece had a finding we loved: traded wide receivers see fewer targets but do more damage with each one, a “fit over volume” story with DeSean Jackson's 2019 return to Philadelphia as the centerpiece. It was true in the first version of our numbers. It is not true in the careful version. Once the comparison players were matched on two prior seasons instead of one, the per-target efficiency gain shrank to nothing. Jackson's season was real; the pattern wasn't. We'd rather tell you that than leave it in.

The same goes for the running-back version of the fresh-start myth. Traded backs as a group did no better than their peers. Traded backs who were handed a bigger job did better, about 1.8 fpts a game ahead of peers, though with only 17 of them that edge doesn't survive our correction for running many comparisons. Think Hillis, or a forgotten Rams bust named Trung Canidate who got Washington's starting job in 2003, ran for nearly 600 yards, and was replaced by Clinton Portis the next spring. His position didn't predict his season. His spot on the depth chart did.

The DeSean Jackson version was more fun and more shareable, and it's the kind of claim that gets repeated on a broadcast for years if nobody checks it against a second season of data. A result that survives only the first, easiest check isn't a finding yet. The role effect in section 02 earns trust because it held up when we tried harder to break it.

How sure are we

Reasonably, with real caveats we'd rather name than bury. This used an actual trade ledger, real birth dates, real depth charts and snap counts, real contracts, and a matched-comparison design (players who look statistically identical except they weren't traded) that we ran three ways and tested against years in which no trade happened. The role result is the one that didn't move no matter how we ran it, including two additional checks past what's shown above: adding a minimum-games floor to rule out small-sample noise, and adding the player's draft round to the matching so pedigree can't be quietly driving the result. Both held: demoted players still trailed their matched peers by roughly 3.0 to 3.5 fpts a game. It's not established the same way for the smallest slices, like running backs or tight ends taken on their own (8 to 10 trades each): the direction points the same way, but the sample is too thin to call it certain.

The overall “trades hurt” number is real too, but softer: peers ahead by somewhere between 0.4 and 1.3 fpts a game depending on exactly how the pre-trade dip is handled, the same structural limit named above.

We also re-checked our confidence intervals a stricter way. Traded players at the same position in the same season can share comparison players, so we redid the math by resampling whole position-and-season groups instead of individual players. That widened the interval for the demoted group (sorted by actual touches) by about 22%, widened the overall trade effect by about 3%, and left the depth-chart demotion interval essentially unchanged. None of the demotion intervals came close to crossing zero.

We can't fully separate the average effect from the dip continuing on its own, which is why the role finding, not the average, is the one to hang your hat on. And none of this is proof in the randomized-experiment sense: no one ran an experiment where teams were assigned to demote players at random. It's the best a real trade ledger and real depth charts can tell you, and the role effect is the part of it that predicted the outcome most consistently across every version of the analysis.

The biggest caveat is who's missing. Of the 1,222 trades in the ledger, only 243 had enough surrounding data (two seasons of stats before the trade, a season after, and enough similar players to match against) to be usable in this analysis; that's the sample the numbers above describe. The obvious worry is that the dropped trades are the ones that flopped. We went and looked, and the reassuring part is that most of them never had a career to flop from: of the 864 trades that fell out at the first cut, 766 involve players who had never recorded an NFL stat season before the trade: draft-day swaps, camp bodies, practice-squad churn. Only 85 are players who had a real prior season and then no season after the trade: established before the trade, then simply absent afterward, with no way yet to tell how much of that is retirement, injury, a roster cut, or just a data window that ends too soon.

Until that's sorted out, treat those 85 as an open question, not as evidence either for or against what's above.

Three more things belong on this list, named plainly rather than checked off: we have not adjusted for how the league itself changed across a 24-season window (rule changes and the shift toward passing change what a typical fpts total looks like, though every comparison is made within the same season), we don't know a player's injury status at the moment of the trade (a demotion could also be a team benching a player it already knew was hurt, which would change the story for those players), and we haven't separated out contract-year incentives, since a player entering a walk year has his own reasons, unrelated to how well he fits his new team, to play through pain or to be benched. These are open, not resolved; we are not claiming to have ruled any of them out.

§

So: not a blessing in disguise. Not a curse. The trade is the delivery mechanism. What arrives is a job, and the team that made the deal already knows how big it is: it's on the depth chart and it's in the contract. Most of this study is some version of that sentence, though not all of it. Plenty of trades in our sample landed as a wash, a player who got roughly the role he had before and produced roughly what he would have anyway; the job only moves the needle when the job itself moves. Hillis and Tebow left the same building two years apart, and that's the version that's easy to check: the city, the fresh air, the new locker aren't what the numbers track. The job is.

One was handed the ball. The other was handed a clipboard with a nicer press release.
Data: nflverse (stats, players, depth charts, snap counts, injuries), nflverse/nfldata (trade ledger, game results, draft picks), OverTheCap contracts via nflverse. Method: real trades only, matched against non-traded peers of the same position, season, age (±2) and prior production, controlling for change in team offensive efficiency; two-season pre-trade baseline; placebo-tested.
