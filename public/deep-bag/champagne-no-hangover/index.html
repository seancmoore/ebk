Champagne, No Hangover

Champions get worse the year after a title, and so do the teams they most resemble. Across 352 title winners in four leagues, champions in the NBA and NHL, and probably the NFL, fell less far than equally good teams that didn’t win.

The Florida Panthers played 68 playoff games across the springs of 2023, 2024 and 2025, and won the Stanley Cup in the last two of them. In 2025-26 they won 40 games, seven fewer than the year before, without their captain, Aleksander Barkov, who missed the whole season. In the spring of 2026 they watched the playoffs from home.

Hangover was the word for it, as it usually is when a champion slips. The theory is old and plausible: a long run eats the offseason, and the bill comes due a year later.

Regression to the mean

A team that wins 13 games usually caught some bounces. Next year a few go the other way, so very good teams drift back toward average whether they won anything or not. The better the season, the bigger the expected drift.

Testing the bill takes a lot of seasons. The data behind this piece rebuild every team-season in four leagues from game and standings records, 7,785 of them: the NFL since 1970, the NBA since 1947, MLB since 1903 and the NHL since 1927. All 356 champions, and every team they beat in the final, were checked against the published record books, and 352 of those champions have a next season to measure.

On average, every league’s champions do get worse the next year. That proves little by itself, since very good teams that won nothing get worse too, so the useful yardstick is equally good teams that didn’t win. Against it, no league showed a clear hangover, and in three of the four the champions held up better. Modern baseball is the exception worth watching, and it gets its own turn near the end.

The fall everyone expects

The first benchmark is plain arithmetic. For each league, a model fitted on every team that did not reach the final predicts next season’s record from this season’s record and scoring margin. Ask it about a team as good as each champion and it returns the drop regression alone would produce. The chart sets that expected drop beside what the champions actually did, in wins on today’s schedules, and the space between each pale bar and the gold one under it is the hangover question in miniature.

NBA champions gave back 3.3 wins on average, where the model expected 6.5 from teams that good. NHL champions dropped 2.0 against an expected 4.4, and NFL champions 1.8 against 2.9. Baseball is the exception in the other direction: World Series winners lost 7.8 wins, almost exactly the 7.5 the benchmark called for.

The 2024-25 Oklahoma City Thunder make a clean example. They won 68 games and the title, then won 64 in 2025-26. Four fewer wins reads like a hangover until you learn the model expected a team that good to give back about six.

Champion by champion, the benchmark turns out to be too gloomy more often than not.

Every era, every league but one

An average over a century can hide a lot, so the grid below splits the same gap by when the title was won. Twenty-year eras run left to right and the leagues run back to front, with baseball nearest. A bar above the glass means that era’s champions beat their benchmark the next season; a bar hanging below means they fell short. The gold front row is the only one that dips below the glass as it reaches the present.

Outside baseball, the decade barely matters. All twelve era averages for the NBA, NHL and NFL sit above the glass, from the postwar Minneapolis Lakers through the Thunder, although several are too noisy to call on their own. Baseball’s row tilts. World Series winners before 1960 landed above their benchmark, the 1940s and 1950s clearly so. Since 1980 they have come in below it, by 3.6 wins a season in the 1980s and 1990s and by 2.6 since 2000. Neither of those two numbers can be told apart from zero, and the pattern only showed up after looking at the data. It comes back near the end anyway.

Twins without rings

The second benchmark skips the model and uses real teams. Each champion gets five twins: the teams from the same league, within five seasons either side, whose record and scoring margin looked most like its own, leaving out anyone who reached the final and the champion’s own franchise. The Thunder’s five were the 2024-25 Cavaliers, the 2024-25 Celtics, the 2021-22 Suns, the 2022-23 Celtics and the 2023-24 Timberwolves. On average they lost 7.2 wins the following year. The Thunder lost four.

Wins, rescaled

Every win figure here is on today’s schedule: 17 NFL games, 82 in the NBA and NHL, 162 in MLB. A 1950s hockey team’s win percentage is translated to an 82-game season, so eras and strike years sit on one scale.

Across the whole sample, NBA champions finished about 2.8 wins ahead of their twins, and 2.6 ahead when the twins also had to match the champion’s season before the title. NHL champions came out 2.8 ahead, shrinking to 1.5 on that stricter match. NFL champions were about a win better, an edge that slips into the noise under the two-season match and clears zero in 9 of 16 ways of picking the twins. Baseball was flat: −0.4 and −0.7 wins over 162 games, and a loss of up to about three wins can’t be ruled out.

What the playoffs know

The twins comparison flatters champions a little, and one pair of hockey teams shows why. The 2022-23 New Jersey Devils and the 2023-24 Florida Panthers had nearly identical regular seasons; the bottom line of each card is where they part.

Same 52 wins, nearly the same goal margin. The Devils won one playoff round and dropped 14 wins the next season. The Panthers won the Cup, dropped five, and won the Cup again. Four rounds of playoff hockey against good teams say something a regular-season record can’t, so a champion measured against regular-season twins has passed four extra exams its twins never sat. The likeliest reading of the twin edge is that a title reveals a team that was already better than its record.

It doesn’t account for all of it. Match champions with teams that ended the season at almost the same Elo rating, which does count playoff games, and the NBA edge shrinks only from 3.0 wins to 2.4, while the NFL’s barely moves, 1.3 to 1.2. If the playoffs explained everything, both should shrink more. The check has limits: Elo ratings exist in this data only for football and basketball, and only about six in ten of those champions have a match that close, mostly the less dominant ones.

The title itself

The twins tell you what a title run revealed. They can’t tell you what the title cost, which is the actual hangover question, and for that you need teams that looked as good after the playoffs as the champion did. The grid below sets both kinds of comparison side by side. The first two columns are the regular-season twins. The other four hold playoff evidence equal: the runner-up the champion beat, teams that went just as deep (counted two ways), and, in football and basketball, teams that finished at the same Elo. Baseball is the front row again, and the gold bars are the main twins comparison.

The twins columns stand clearly above the glass everywhere but baseball. The other four turn hazy. In basketball and baseball the title-only estimates land on both sides of zero, and no title-only estimate in any league can be told apart from zero. Hockey comes closest to a pattern: champions came out ahead in every title-only comparison, by 0.8 to 1.6 wins, including +1.6 against the team they beat in the final. NFL champions never come out behind in one either. In neither league is that strong enough to call, and in hockey how much of it survives depends on how you count playoff rounds across formats that had two, three or four of them.

The Super Bowl loser curse

The runner-up version of the myth may be even more popular: lose the Super Bowl and next season is cursed. The strip below lists every Super Bowl loser since the 1970 season in order, filled if the team made the next postseason, in two rows that break at 1987.

Through the 1986 season, all 17 made it back. Since then 17 of 38 have missed, the 2023 49ers and the 2024 Chiefs among them. That looks like a curse until you check teams that were just as good and never reached the Super Bowl. Since 1987 they made the next postseason 54% of the time. Super Bowl losers made it 55% of the time.

The Chiefs are the case people will cite, and they show both what the math catches and what it misses. Kansas City went 15-2 in 2024 while outscoring opponents by only 59 points, 385 to 326, and won all 11 of its one-score games. That profile screams regression, and the benchmark said to expect about 5.5 fewer wins. They lost nine. The extra 3.5 is an unusual fall: only about one Super Bowl team in eight has dropped that far below its benchmark.

Look at how it happened, though. The 6-11 Chiefs of 2025 outscored their opponents, 362 to 328. They went 1-9 in one-score games, and their season ended with Patrick Mahomes tearing his ACL. The close games that broke their way in 2024 broke the other way a year later, and then the quarterback got hurt.

Runners-up in every league track their own twins within about a win either way. Over all 55 seasons, Super Bowl losers made the next playoffs more often than comparable teams, 69% to 54%, and nearly all of that gap comes from the years before 1987.

Sixty-eight games in three springs

If a title costs anything, fatigue is the likeliest mechanism, and it makes a testable prediction: champions who needed more games to get there should fall further. An NHL champion that goes seven games in every round plays twelve more than one that sweeps, and starts its summer weeks later. So each champion’s extra games, the playoff losses a sweeper never has, were tested as a predictor of a worse next season than the benchmark. The chart covers the three leagues where series length varies. A bar left of zero would mean longer runs were followed by bigger falls.

Baseball is flat and basketball close to it. Hockey carries a hint, about 0.58 fewer wins next season for every extra playoff game, but the interval runs from −1.38 to +0.16. For a champion that needed ten extra games, the data allow anything from about 14 wins worse to 2 better, which leaves room for a large fatigue cost and for none at all.

Florida fits the hint. After those 68 games the Panthers finished 3.6 wins below what the benchmark expected for 2025-26. About one NHL champion in seven has fallen that far below the math, though, and so have 30% of teams that never got near a final.

Repeat customers

A hangover should also make champions worse at winning again. To check, each champion got a forecast of how often it would repeat if titles went purely by regular-season quality, run against the whole next-season field and simulated 100,000 times. Each league gets two bars below: the repeat rate quality alone predicts, with the middle 95% of the simulations, and the rate that actually happened.

Hockey stands out. NHL champions repeated 27 times in 98 tries, against 16.8 expected from quality alone, and the gap holds up even when each dynasty is treated as a single data point. Most of it came after the 1967 expansion, 15 repeats where the model expected 6.2: the Canadiens in 1969 and three more times in the late 1970s, the Flyers, the Islanders’ four straight, the Oilers and Penguins twice each, the Red Wings, the Lightning, and the Panthers in 2025. Our untested guess is a playoff skill, goaltending perhaps, that regular-season numbers understate.

The NBA looks similar at first, 23 repeats against 15.0 expected, until you notice that 8 of them belong to Bill Russell’s Celtics. Without them it’s 15 against 11.0, well within chance. Football and baseball champions repeat a little more often than their quality predicts, also within chance; the Chiefs did it in the 2023 season and the Dodgers in 2025. In no league do champions repeat less often than their quality says they should.

Fire sales and retirements

Some champions do collapse. Measured against what the benchmark expected, the biggest drops in the data mostly look like the 1997 Florida Marlins, who went 92-70, won the World Series, and went 54-108 the next year after ownership sold off the roster, 32.5 wins worse than expected. The 1998 Bulls won a sixth title at 62-20, then lost Michael Jordan to retirement, broke up the roster and went 13-37 in the lockout season, 31.9 wins below expectation on an 82-game scale. The 1969 Celtics won their 11th title in 13 years and went 34-48 after Bill Russell retired. The 1998 Broncos repeated at 14-2 and went 6-10 once John Elway retired.

Those were demolitions, with a retirement or a fire sale doing the work, and none of them needs a hangover to explain it. A few big drops come without a clean story (the 2013 Red Sox simply fell apart, and the 1917 White Sox lost players to the First World War), and the other tail exists too: the 2015 Warriors went 67-15, won the title, and then went 73-9. The typical champion keeps its team together. In baseball, where the player files let us check, champions brought back players who had produced 84.6% of their title-season playing time, against 79.5% for their twins, and they were no older.

How sure we are

We are confident about the direction and much less confident about the size. Pool all four leagues and the edge over twins works out to about 2 wins a season in the NBA, a bit less in the NHL and half a win in the NFL. Baseball on its own shows none, but the leagues don’t disagree with one another by more than chance allows. No single league clears our strictest bar by itself once you account for how many comparisons we ran; the NHL comes closest.

Two wrinkles. The NBA edge shows up more in wins than in scoring margin, and roughly 40% of it, give or take a lot, comes from champions winning more close games the next year than their point differentials would predict. That could be late-game skill or luck, and the data can’t say which. In baseball, switch off the statistical correction for imperfect twins and two versions of the test show World Series winners falling about 2.5 wins further than their twins. We read that as a flaw in the uncorrected test, since the best champions have no truly equal twins and better teams fall further. With the correction on, the same two tests give −0.7 and −0.6.

The bigger limit is the one from the title-only grid. To put a ceiling on what a title could cost, take the least favourable comparison in each league that holds playoff evidence equal, and read off the largest drop its 95% interval still allows. The chart sets that worst case against the drop a team that good should expect anyway, first over each league’s whole history and then for titles since 1980.

Over each league’s full history the worst case is the smaller number every time: 1.1 wins against a normal drop of 2.9 in the NFL, 4.8 against 6.5 in the NBA, 1.8 against 4.4 in hockey and, only just, 6.8 against 7.5 in baseball. As a share of a season, that worst case runs from 2% in the NHL to 7% in the NFL.

The right-hand panel is where baseball breaks. Since 1980, World Series winners have come out 3 to 8 wins worse than teams that went just as far, depending on the comparison. Where you start the modern era hardly matters (1969, 1985 and 1995 give the same picture), and from 1985 on even the cleanest version, champion against the team it beat in the Series, clears zero. It is the one place where the worst case, about 14 wins, is bigger than the normal drop of about 6. The NBA since 1980 is close to a tie, 7.2 against 7.6.

It is still a lead and no more. The split was chosen after seeing the data, each slice holds only 30 to 55 champions, and once you correct for every era split tried across four leagues, it no longer clears our bar. If a title hangover exists anywhere in these four sports, modern baseball is where to look next.

The subtraction is easy to do at home. Regression alone takes back about 3 wins from a typical NFL champion, 4 from an NHL champion, 6 or 7 in the NBA and 7 or 8 in baseball (closer to 6 since 1990). Champions usually beat that by a little: about a win in the NFL, three in the NBA, two in the NHL and none in baseball. Single teams scatter widely around it, and a normal miss is about 3 wins in the NFL, 7 in the NHL, 9 in the NBA and 10 in baseball, in either direction.

As of October 2026, the reigning champions offer three different setups. The Seahawks went 14-3 in the 2025 season and beat teams by 11 points a game, so regression alone says about 3 fewer wins in 2026, and a typical champion gives back about 2. The Knicks won only 53 games in 2025-26 and outscored teams by 6 points a night, which leaves less to give back: regression says about 2, and a typical NBA champion in their spot would gain about 1. Baseball’s 2026 title is still being decided this month; the Dodgers, champions in 2024 and 2025, hold it until then.

Carolina is the one to watch. The Hurricanes went 53-22-7 in 2025-26 while outscoring teams by only 0.7 goals a night, the profile of a team that won its share of coin flips, so regression says about 6 fewer wins and a typical champion about 4. Florida dropped seven in 2025-26 and got called hungover for it. Regression has Carolina at about 47 wins in 2026-27, the same total Florida posted the year before its own slide.

Every champion and runner-up was checked against Wikipedia’s published lists; 2025-26 facts and the NHL records quoted here against a second source.

Method. Expected drops come from league-by-league regressions of next season on this season, fitted on teams that didn’t reach the final. Twins are the five nearest non-finalists within five seasons on win percentage and scoring margin (in the second method, the previous season too), excluding the champion’s own franchise and matching shortened seasons only with shortened seasons, with a bias adjustment; intervals account for the same twin serving more than one champion. NBA and NHL seasons are named by the year they end, NFL and MLB by the year they’re played. No payroll data went into any of this, and roster data exist only for baseball.
