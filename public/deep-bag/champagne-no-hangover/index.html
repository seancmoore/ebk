EBK Deep Bag

Champagne,
No Hangover

We followed every champion in four leagues into the season after the parade. They come back to earth. In the NBA and NHL, and probably the NFL, they don’t fall as far as anybody else that good.

A Deep Bag investigation · October 2026
356 champions, 352 followed into the next season · 7,785 team-seasons · 1903–2026

The Kansas City Chiefs won back-to-back Super Bowls, went 15-2 chasing a third, lost the big game, and then went 6-11. This past spring the Florida Panthers, winners of the two previous Stanley Cups, missed the playoffs entirely, after playing 68 playoff games in three springs. You know the words people reach for. Hangover. Tired legs. Short summer. The curse.

It’s the most intuitive idea in sports: winning it all costs you something. You play extra games, you party, you lose players you can’t afford to pay, and next year you pay for it. Every fall someone writes the column.

So we checked. We built every team-season in the NFL (since 1970), NBA (since 1947), MLB (since 1903) and NHL (since 1927) from game and standings data, and checked all 356 champions, and every team they beat in the final, against the record books. 352 of those champions have a next season to study. Then we asked a sharper question than “did they get worse?” Almost everybody that good gets worse. The real question is whether champions get worse than equally good teams that didn’t win.

We couldn’t find a league where they clearly do. In most, champions actually hold up better. What the data can’t do is prove a title costs nothing at all, so at the end we’ll show you the biggest hangover that could still be hiding, why it’s smaller than you’d think, and the one sport where we’re not so sure. Along the way we’ll come back to the Chiefs and the Panthers: one fall that really was unusual, and one that mostly wasn’t.

01 Everybody falls. That’s the trap.

Start with the thing that fools everyone: regression to the mean. A team that wins 13 games usually got a few bounces. Next year some of those bounces go the other way. The better your season, the further you tend to fall, title or no title.

So we built the benchmark first. For each league, using only teams that did not reach the final, we modeled how next season’s record depends on this season’s record and point differential (runs in baseball, goals in hockey). Then we compared what that model predicts for champions with what they actually did.

Champions do fall. NBA champions give back 3.3 wins on average, but the model says a team that good should give back 6.5. NHL champions drop 2.0 against an expected 4.4; NFL champions 1.8 against 2.9. Baseball is the one league where champions land almost exactly on the line: 7.8 wins lost, 7.5 expected.

In 2024-25 the Thunder won 68 games and the title. Last season they won 64. Four fewer wins sounds like a hangover, but the math said they should lose 6. The drop everyone calls a hangover is arithmetic, and in three leagues champions don’t even fall as far as the arithmetic says.

That’s the average, and individual teams scatter around it. 37% of champions did finish below the model’s line. If the model were exactly right about champions, about half would. A real curse would push that well above half.

02 Champions vs. their twins

A model is one check. The more intuitive one is a lineup. For every champion we found the five teams from the same league and era whose record and point differential looked most like theirs, leaving out anyone who reached the final and the champion’s own franchise, and compared what happened next. The Thunder’s five closest twins (the 2025 Cavaliers, 2025 Celtics, 2022 Suns, 2023 Celtics and 2024 Timberwolves) lost 7.2 wins on average the next season. The Thunder lost 4.

We ran the lineup two ways: twins matched on the title season alone, and twins matched on the title season and the season before it. The chart shows both. Anything right of zero means champions held up better than their twins.

NBA champions finished next season about 2.8 wins ahead of their twins, and 2.6 with the stricter two-season match. NHL champions, 2.8, shrinking to 1.5. NFL champions, about one win (+1.0), and that one wobbles: with the two-season match it slips back into the noise, and it clears zero in 9 of 16 ways of picking the twins. Baseball is flat: −0.4 and −0.7 wins over a 162-game season, both ranges including zero. The estimates lean slightly negative, and a loss of up to about 3 wins can’t be excluded.

A title isn’t a curse. If anything, it’s a sign the team was better than its record.

03 What the playoffs tell you

In 2022-23 the New Jersey Devils went 52-22-8 and outscored opponents by 65 goals. A year later the Florida Panthers went 52-24-6 and outscored them by 68. Near twins: 52 wins each, about 0.8 goals a night. The Devils won one playoff round. The Panthers won the Cup. Next season the Devils won 14 fewer games. The Panthers won 5 fewer, and then won the Cup again.

Four rounds of playoff hockey against good teams is information the regular-season record doesn’t have. Compare a champion with its regular-season twins and you’re comparing a team that passed four extra exams with teams that never sat them. No wonder the champion tends to hold up better. That’s the likeliest reading of the twins gap: not a title making a team better, but a title revealing a team that already was.

It isn’t airtight. Match champions with teams that ended the season with nearly the same Elo power rating, which counts playoff games, and the edge shrinks less than that story predicts. For the same champions it goes from 3.0 wins over their regular-season twins to 2.4 in the NBA, and hardly moves in the NFL. We have those ratings only for football and basketball, and only about six in ten of their champions have a match that close, so the check covers the less dominant ones.

The season before is the other thing the one-season record misses. Champions’ twins had, on average, risen faster into that season, and one-year risers fall hardest. Matching on two seasons trims the NHL edge by almost half and barely moves the NBA’s.

There’s a catch, and it cuts the other way. If the twins gap is mostly information, it can’t tell you whether the title itself costs something.

For that you have to compare champions with teams that looked equally good after the playoffs: the runner-up they beat, or teams that went just as deep. Those comparisons are much blurrier, and in basketball and baseball they land on both sides of zero. In hockey and football, champions come out ahead in every one of them (in hockey by 0.8 to 1.6 wins, including +1.6 against the team they beat in the final), though never by enough to rule out zero.

So something beyond playoff depth may be going on there. In hockey, how much depends on how you count playoff rounds across formats that had two, three or four of them.

04 The Super Bowl loser curse isn’t one

The runner-up version of the myth is even more popular. Lose the Super Bowl and your team is supposedly doomed. Here is every Super Bowl loser since 1970, filled in if it made the next postseason.

Through 1986, no Super Bowl loser missed the next postseason. Since 1987, 17 of 38 have, including the 2023 49ers and those 2024 Chiefs. That sounds like a curse until you check teams just as good that didn’t reach the Super Bowl: they made the next postseason 54% of the time. Super Bowl losers: 55%. Dead even.

The Chiefs show how the math works, and where it runs out. They went 15-2 but outscored opponents by only 59 points, the profile of a team that won a lot of coin flips: they were 11-0 in one-score games. Regression said to expect about 5.5 fewer wins. They lost 9, so roughly 3.5 wins of the fall went beyond the math. Only about one Super Bowl team in eight has fallen that far below its benchmark, so that one really was unusual.

But look at how it happened. The 6-11 Chiefs outscored their opponents, 362 to 328. They went 1-9 in one-score games, and the season ended with Patrick Mahomes tearing his ACL. That’s the coin flips landing the other way, plus an injury. It isn’t a curse.

Runners-up in every league track their twins, within about a win either way. Across all 55 seasons, Super Bowl losers actually made the playoffs more often than equally good teams (69% vs. 54%), and almost all of that gap comes from before 1987. The losers of the big game just get noticed.

05 Does a long run wear you out?

The fatigue version of the theory makes a specific prediction: champions who needed more games to win should fall further. An NHL champion that goes seven games in every round plays twelve more games than one that sweeps, and starts its summer weeks later.

So for each champion we counted extra games: playoff losses, which a sweeper doesn’t have. Then we asked whether they predicted a worse next season than regression expects. The chart shows the answer for the three leagues where series length varies.

Mostly, nothing. The baseball slope is flat and the NBA’s is small, with ranges on both sides of zero. Hockey is the one place with a hint: about 0.58 fewer wins next season per extra playoff game. But the range runs from −1.38 to +0.16, so for a champion that needed ten extra games, the data allow anything from about 14 wins worse to 2 better. That’s not a small effect. It’s an unmeasured one.

Florida fits the hint: 68 playoff games in three springs, then a season 3.6 wins worse than expected, with captain Aleksander Barkov out all year. But that shortfall is ordinary. About one NHL champion in seven has fallen that far below the math, and so have 30% of teams that never got near a final. One team is a story, not a slope.

06 Champions repeat more, not less

If titles were handed out purely on regular-season quality, how often would a champion repeat? We built that forecast from each league’s history, pitting every champion against the whole next-season field, and simulated it 100,000 times.

NHL champions repeated 27 times in 98 tries, against 16.8 expected from quality alone, and the gap holds up even when you treat each dynasty as a single data point. Most of it came after the 1967 expansion (15 repeats where the model expected 6.2): the Flyers, the Canadiens in 1969 and three more times in the late ’70s, the Islanders’ four straight, the Oilers, the Penguins twice, the Red Wings, the Lightning, and the Panthers in 2025. Our untested guess is a playoff skill, like goaltending, that regular-season numbers understate.

The NBA looks similar at first (23 repeats against 15.0 expected), but 8 of those belong to Bill Russell’s Celtics. Without them it’s 15 against 11.0, well within chance. NFL and MLB champions repeat a bit more often than their quality predicts, also within chance. The Chiefs did it in 2023, the Dodgers in 2025. A hangover would push these numbers down. None of them go down.

07 The collapses you remember

Of course some champions fall off a cliff. Most of the biggest drops in the data, measured against what regression expected, look like this:

1997 Marlins92-70 and a World Series, then 54-108 after the ownership sold off the roster. −32.5 wins vs. expectation (162-game scale). 1998 Bulls62-20 and a sixth title, then 13-37 in the lockout-shortened season. Jordan retired and the roster was broken up. −31.9 wins vs. expectation (82-game scale). 1969 Celtics48-34 and an 11th title in 13 years, then 34-48 after Bill Russell retired. −17.0 wins vs. expectation (82-game scale). 1998 Broncos14-2 and a second straight Super Bowl, then 6-10 after John Elway retired. −4.7 wins vs. expectation (17-game scale).

These are demolitions, not hangovers: a retirement, a fire sale, a roster that was always going to be broken up. Not every big drop has a clean story (the 2013 Red Sox just fell apart, and the 1917 White Sox lost players to World War I), but the famous ones do. And the other tail exists too: the 2015 Warriors went 67-15, won the title, then went 73-9.

The typical champion keeps its team together. In baseball, where the player files let us measure it, champions brought back players who had produced 84.6% of their title-season playing time, against 79.5% for their twins, and they were not older.

08 How sure are we?

Sure of the direction. Less sure of the size, and least sure about the title itself.

The direction holds everywhere we looked. Put all four leagues together and the pooled edge over twins works out to about 2 wins a season in the NBA, a bit less in the NHL and half a win in the NFL. Baseball on its own shows none, but the leagues don’t disagree with each other more than chance would allow. What doesn’t hold is any single league on its own. We ran a lot of comparisons, and once you account for that, none clears our strictest bar by itself. The NHL comes closest.

Two wrinkles. The NBA edge shows up in wins more than in point differential, and when we checked, about 40% of it (give or take a lot) is champions winning more close games the next year than their margins would predict. That could be late-game skill or luck; the data can’t say which.

And baseball has a crack. Switch off the correction for imperfect twins and two versions of the test show World Series winners falling about 2.5 wins further than their twins. We think that’s a flaw in the test, not a hangover: the best champions have no truly equal twins, and better teams fall further. With the correction on, it’s −0.7. But it’s there, so we’re telling you.

The bigger limit is the one from section 03. Twins show what the playoffs revealed. They can’t show what the title itself cost. For that you need teams that were just as good after the playoffs, and those comparisons are blurry. So we took the worst case each league allows and held it up against the drop a team that good should expect anyway:

Over each league’s full history, the worst case is smaller: 1.1 wins against a normal 2.9 in the NFL, 4.8 against 6.5 in the NBA, 1.8 against 4.4 in hockey, and, only just, 6.8 against 7.5 in baseball. Even if a title does cost something, most of the fall is still arithmetic.

Baseball is the close call, and it has an asterisk. Since 1980, World Series winners have come out 3 to 8 wins worse than teams that went just as far. It doesn’t matter much where you start the modern era (1969, 1985 or 1995 give the same picture), and from 1985 on even the cleanest version, champion against the team it beat, clears zero. It’s the one place where the worst case, about 14 wins, is bigger than the normal drop of about 6.

But we went looking for it after seeing the data, each slice holds only 30 to 55 champions, and once you correct for every era split we tried across four leagues, it no longer clears our bar. So it’s a lead, not a finding. If a title hangover exists anywhere, modern baseball is where to look.

09 The verdict

Champions get worse. So does everybody that good. Over the long run, no league shows champions falling further than equally good teams, and in three they fall less, probably because a title run was telling you something true about them. On average, a World Series title tells you nothing about next season that the standings didn’t. The Super Bowl loser curse is just what happens to very good NFL teams. And playoff fatigue, if it exists, is too faint to see.

We can’t prove a title costs nothing. Outside modern baseball, we can say the most it could cost is smaller than the drop a team that good owes the math anyway, and we found no evidence it costs anything at all. Modern baseball is the one place we’d keep watching.

Which leaves one number per league worth carrying into next season:

So do the subtraction. Regression alone takes back about 3 wins in the NFL, 4 in the NHL, 6 or 7 in the NBA and 7 or 8 in baseball (closer to 6 since 1990). It’s more for a team that won a lot of close games, and less for one that didn’t win that many to begin with.

Single teams scatter widely around it: a normal miss is about 3 wins in the NFL, 7 in the NHL, 9 in the NBA and 10 in baseball, either way. Lose less than the math says and it isn’t a hangover. It’s a good team. Lose a lot more and look for a torn ACL, a missing captain or a run of bad luck before you blame the parade.

This year’s champions show how it works. The Seahawks went 14-3 and beat people by 11 points a game, so regression alone says about 3 fewer wins, and a typical champion gives back about 2. The Knicks won only 53 and outscored teams by 6 points a night, so there’s less to give back: regression says about 2, and a typical NBA champion would actually gain about 1.

The Hurricanes are the ones to watch. They went 53-22-7 but outscored teams by only 0.7 goals a night, the profile of a team that won its share of coin flips, so regression says about 6 fewer wins and a typical champion about 4. If Carolina drops six this winter, you’ll hear the word hangover. Now you know what to subtract.

How we did it: team-season records and playoff results built from FiveThirtyEight and nflverse game data (NFL), FiveThirtyEight and ESPN via sportsdataverse (NBA), the Lahman database and MLB Stats API (MLB), and the NHL’s stats API (NHL); every champion and runner-up checked against Wikipedia’s published lists. “Expected” drops come from league-by-league regressions of next season on this season, fitted on non-finalists. Twins are the five nearest non-finalists within five seasons on win% and point differential (and, in the second method, the previous season too), excluding the champion’s own franchise and matching shortened seasons only with shortened seasons, with a statistical adjustment for any remaining gap. Ranges account for the same twin serving more than one champion. The worst-case hangover is the lowest end of the 95% ranges across the comparisons that hold playoff evidence equal, one comparison at a time. NBA and NHL seasons are named by the year they end; NFL seasons by the year they start. Hockey records are shown as in the official standings (wins, losses, overtime and shootout losses); in the analysis an overtime or shootout loss counts as a loss, and goal totals leave out shootout-deciding goals. Facts about the 2025-26 seasons and the NHL records quoted here were checked against a second source (Wikipedia season pages). No payroll data went into any of this, and roster data exist only for baseball. The full technical report has every formula, table and robustness check.
