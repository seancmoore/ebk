Home Court Is Shrinking. Denver Still Has One.

On a straight line through the seasons, NBA home teams won 61.7% of games in 2002 and 55.3% in 2026. The NFL slide could be noise, the empty arenas of 2020-21 took the edge away, and Denver, a mile above sea level, still has the biggest one.

On March 16, 2008, Denver beat the Seattle SuperSonics 168 to 116. The Sonics are the franchise that plays in Oklahoma City today, and ESPN’s record of the game files the visitor under OKC. The Nuggets had 19,155 people behind them. The 52-point margin is the biggest home win Denver has had in the 25 NBA seasons behind this piece, and 168 is the most points it scored in its own building.

That team finished the season 33-8 at home and 17-24 on the road. At home it won by an average of 10.5 points a game. Away it lost by 3.1. Set against how the rest of the league does, that swing was 6.7 points bigger than the league’s usual gap between home and road. Which raises the question this study started with: is an edge like that, the lift a team gets from playing in its own building, going away?

By home edge we mean how much better a team does in its own building than it does elsewhere, counted two ways: how often the home team wins, and by how many points. We measured it in the NFL from 1999 to 2025 and in the NBA from 2002 to 2026, using every regular-season game with a true home team, 6,901 in the NFL and 29,947 in the NBA. Baseball and hockey are missing, because the data on hand has no game results for them.

Two ways to count

Win rate is the share of games the home team wins. The point edge is the average amount the home team outscores the visitor by, so a positive number means the home side is ahead on average. A team’s own edge compares its home form with its road form.

The short version has four parts. The NBA’s home edge has clearly shrunk. The NFL’s has probably slipped a little, though not by an amount we can tell apart from luck. The empty arenas of 2020-21 show that the crowd was a large part of the edge. And the biggest lasting edges in the NBA belong to Denver and Utah, for reasons nobody has pinned down. Altitude is the popular guess, and a section near the end says why it is only a guess.

The NBA edge got smaller

Across all normal NBA seasons, home teams won 58.6% of their games and outscored visitors by 2.78 points. That average hides a slide. A straight line fitted through the seasons runs from a 61.7% home win rate in 2002 to 55.3% in 2026. The line starts higher than the 2002 dot because it is the best fit through every normal season, not any one year. The chart below plots each season as a dot, with the fitted line dashed beside it. Watch the NBA line against the 50% mark, a coin flip: it sits well above it on the left and has drifted toward it.

Out of every 82 home games, the home team now wins about 5.2 fewer than it did at the start (plausible range 2.0 to 8.4). For one team’s 41 home games, that is 2.6 fewer wins a season. In points, the edge fell from about 3.65 to 1.87, a drop of 1.78 (range −3.04 to −0.56).

5.2

fewer home wins out of every 82 home games in the NBA, 2026 against 2002, on the straight-line trend. The plausible range is 2.0 to 8.4.

The slide is real over the whole stretch, but it is lumpier than a straight line suggests. Stop at 2019 and the trend is about −1.7 points of win rate per decade, with a plausible range of −4.3 to +0.9 that includes no change at all. Much of the drop is a lower level since 2020: the 2022 to 2026 seasons averaged 55.3%. The game itself also changed. The typical spread of final margins widened from 12.8 points in the first ten seasons to 14.6 in the last ten, and in a game with more blowouts the same point edge wins fewer games.

The NFL slide could be noise

Football looks like a weaker version of the same story. NFL home teams won 56.5% of their regular-season games and outscored visitors by 2.36 points. The fitted line runs from 58.3% in 1999 to 54.6% in 2025, a slide of about 1.4 points of win rate per decade, but the plausible range for that slope is −3.3 to +0.5, which includes zero. When we reshuffle the seasons to see how steady the line is, 93.0% of the reshuffled lines slope downward. That makes a decline likely and not established.

A few facts sit awkwardly with a clean fade. The NFL’s low point came before the pandemic: 2019 was already down at 52.0%, with home teams outscored by 0.05 points on average. And the five most recent seasons, 2021 to 2025, averaged a 2.06-point edge and a 53.9% home win rate. That is below the long-run 56.5%, and it is not a collapse.

It is real, and the market knows

Home teams could win more simply because the better team happens to be at home. They do not, and the check is worth spelling out. For the NFL we measured team quality with the closing point spread. For the NBA we gave each team a rating built from every other game of that season, so a game never helps rate its own teams. With quality accounted for, the home edge is 2.36 points in the NFL (plausible range 2.04 to 2.68) and 2.78 in the NBA (2.66 to 2.90). The adjustment barely moves the averages, because schedules are balanced and good and bad teams take turns at home. What it shows is that the edge belongs to the building: in the NFL it is 1.8 points for big home underdogs and 2.9 for big home favorites.

Oddsmakers noticed the slide. The home-field allowance built into their own spreads fell from 2.51 points in 1999 to 2009 to 1.63 in 2021 to 2025, while the real edge in those two stretches went from 2.59 to 2.06. Averaged across every game since 1999, the gap between what happened and what the line expected is +0.07 points, which is no gap at all.

The season the stands emptied

Then came an experiment nobody designed. The NBA played its 2020-21 season mostly in front of empty or nearly empty buildings. For each team we compared its home edge that season with its own average over the five normal seasons before it, 2015 to 2019, so that one great home team could not fake a result. The chart below shows that comparison on the left, with the years after it, and on the right the NBA season split by how full the building really was, using ESPN’s attendance for each game.

In the 574 NBA games of 2020-21 played with zero fans, home teams won 290, or 50.5%, and their average margin was −0.10 points, a hair under even. Against each team’s own normal, that is 2.8 points of home edge lost (plausible range −4.0 to −1.6). Over the whole season the league’s home edge fell 1.67 points compared with 2015 to 2019 (range −2.55 to −0.81). An ordinary year-to-year wobble in that league-wide edge is only 0.33 points, so this sits far outside the ordinary.

Denver’s own building shows what the numbers describe. The two nights below were both big home wins by the Nuggets. One had 19,155 people in the stands. The other, almost thirteen years later, had nobody.

NBA arenas did not flip from empty to full in one step, and that lets us look inside the season. Games with some fans back looked close to normal: under 10% of a normal crowd, +0.5 points of edge (93 games); 10% to 25%, −0.7 (366 games); 25% to 50%, +0.7 (46 games), each with a range wide enough to include zero. Comparing games with fans to games without them inside the same calendar month, which removes the effect of the season wearing on, fans were worth +2.9 points of edge (range 1.0 to 5.0). That reads more like any crowd against no crowd than a smooth dial, but the groups are too small to be sure.

The effect faded as the buildings filled. In 2021-22 the edge was still −0.89 against the 2015 to 2019 normal (range −1.49 to −0.32), with arenas averaging 17,171 fans compared with 18,077 the next year. In 2022-23 it was back to −0.10 (range −0.80 to +0.60). Then 2023-24 through 2025-26 slipped again, to −0.76 (range −1.31 to −0.27). So the empty-arena hit reversed, and the longer decline did not.

The NFL had its own mostly empty season, 2020, and it looks similar: a 50.4% home win rate, a home margin of 0.14 points, and a 1.75-point drop against each team’s own 2015 to 2019 norm (range −3.43 to −0.07). One NFL season is noisy, though. A typical year-to-year wobble is 0.88 points, and the range on 2020’s margin alone runs from −2.3 to +2.6. The market cut its own home allowance to 1.08 points that year. NFL attendance is not in our data, so the NFL result is about 2020 as a whole, and the stadiums that let in some fans late in the year are not separated out. By 2021 the NFL edge was back: −0.28 (range −1.65 to +1.09), and +0.30 for 2021 to 2025 together.

Fans are the leading explanation for all of this, not the only one. That NBA season also had compressed schedules and players missing games with COVID. The bubble gives a hint without settling anything: in the 88 seeding games of the 2019-20 bubble, a nominal home team with no crowd and no travel, the “home” side came out 1.65 points ahead, but the range runs from −1.1 to +4.4, too wide to say.

Some buildings are different

Rank every franchise by how much better it does at home than on the road, compared with the league, and team quality drops out of the comparison. The chart below shows the five biggest and five smallest in each league. In the NBA the top is Denver at +2.1 points above the league’s typical home edge (plausible range 0.9 to 3.3), then Utah at +2.0, Indiana at +1.5 and Portland at +1.2. The smallest are Boston at −1.4, Brooklyn at −1.3 and Philadelphia and New York at −1.2.

NBA franchises differ by more than luck would explain. If every team had the same true home edge, a spread this wide would turn up about 2 times in 10,000. The best estimate of the real spread between franchises is 0.67 points, against 0.91 observed, the rest being noise.

The next question is whether a big edge lasts. We split the data in half and asked whether the teams that stood out in the first half stood out in the second. The chart below follows the five biggest and five smallest of each league's first era to the later one.

In the NBA, yes, partly. On a scale where 0 means no link and 1 means a perfect one, the link between the two eras is 0.49 (range 0.09 to 0.74), and splitting odd and even seasons gives a similar 0.46. The five biggest first-era franchises averaged +1.9 then and +0.7 later; the five smallest averaged −1.5 then and −0.6 later. About 0.39 of a first-era gap carries into the next era. The gaps are shrinking too: our best estimate of the real spread between franchises was 0.84 points in the first era and 0.16 in the later one.

Sacramento, the edge that vanished

Sacramento’s home edge was +1.2 points at Arco and Sleep Train Arena (2002 to 2016) and −1.7 at Golden 1 Center (2017 to 2026). Luck alone would produce a gap that large about 6 times in 1,000.

In the NFL, no. The biggest measured edge belongs to Minnesota at +2.0 (range −0.5 to +4.4) and the smallest to the Giants at −2.7 (range −5.3 to −0.1), but every range overlaps. The spread among NFL franchises is what luck alone would produce, and the early leaders were not leaders later (link −0.29, range −0.58 to +0.09). The five franchises that looked best early, at +3.5 points, averaged −1.9 afterward. Do not tell a fortress story about any NFL stadium on this data.

The mile-high edge

Denver is first of 30 NBA franchises, and Utah, also at high altitude, is its twin. If you shuffled the team labels at random, the top two teams would average 1.12 points above the league. Denver and Utah average 2.01, and luck would produce a gap like that about 5 times in 10,000. The timeline below follows Denver season by season, in points a game above or below the league’s usual gap between home and road. Zero is the league typical, and the labels on the biggest dots are Denver’s home records.

Three seasons carry the story. In 2007-08 Denver went 33-8 at home. In 2018-19 it went 34-7 at home and 20-21 on the road, winning by 10.5 points at home and losing by 2.6 away, a swing 7.7 above the league’s. The season ended with a home loss in Game 7 to Portland, 100 to 96, on May 12, 2019. The biggest swing in the data came in 2022-23: 34-7 at home, 19-22 on the road, margins of +9.8 and −3.1, 7.9 above the league’s. The playoff run that followed is missing from the box-score files we used, so no game from it is quoted here.

Denver’s edge shrank along with the league’s, from +2.4 in the first half of the data to +1.6 in the second. It stayed positive. It also still loses in its own building when it counts: Game 7 at home in 2024 ended 98 to 90 for Minnesota, on May 19.

The usual explanation is altitude, which visiting teams are not used to. That is a hypothesis, and this data does not prove it. We noticed the altitude link after ranking the teams, so the ranking cannot also serve as a test of it. The one direct check we ran, visitors playing the second night of a back-to-back, found they lose by 0.7 more points at Denver or Utah than elsewhere, with a range from −0.7 to +2.0 over 457 games. That is inconclusive. Denver’s empty-arena season does not settle it either. With zero fans at home the Nuggets went 13-9 and beat their rating by 1.5 points, and against every other team’s zero-fan edge they were +1.6 (range −3.6 to +6.8). Twenty-two games cannot separate altitude from crowd. Other teams won 50.2% of their zero-fan home games.

What a fan can use

Two evenly matched teams make the cleanest test of home field. The home side wins 58.7% of NFL games between close matchups (2,285 games, range 56.6 to 60.7) and 59.4% in the NBA (5,773 games, range 58.1 to 60.6). In the latest five seasons those fall to 57.6% in the NFL (range 53.0 to 62.0) and 56.0% in the NBA (range 53.1 to 58.7).

Counted in wins, home games are worth less than they used to be. An NBA team got about 4.3 extra wins a season from its home games in the first ten seasons of the data, 2.7 in the last ten, and 2.2 in the latest five. In the NFL the same measure is 0.62, 0.43 and 0.34 over a team’s 8.5 home games. (Extra wins means home games times the home win rate minus 50%.)

Do not expect to bet on it. NFL home teams covered the closing spread 49.0% of the time over 6,712 games (range 47.8 to 50.2), and road teams 51.0%. Break-even at standard prices is 52.4%, so neither side pays. Home underdogs covered 50.2% over 2,353 games, and in 2021 to 2025 home teams covered 49.3%. The market already knows about home field. In the playoffs, NFL home teams won 64.4% of 281 games and beat the spread by an average of 0.70 points, give or take 0.80, which the 4.6-point average line already reflected. We have no NBA betting lines, and the 2022-23 NBA playoffs are missing from our box-score files, so we make no NBA playoff or seeding claim.

What this cannot tell you

Baseball and hockey are out because the data on hand has no game results for either. NFL attendance is not in it, so there is no crowd-size test for 2020. We ran many comparisons across two sports, several time windows and team tables, so the large results (the NBA slide, the empty arenas, the Denver and Utah ranking) deserve more weight than the small ones. Single NFL seasons, the bubble games and any single NFL franchise’s rank should not be quoted as findings. The plausible ranges treat home teams as independent within a season and ignore shared opponents and travel. The adjusted NBA ranges treat each team’s rating as known, so they are narrower than the raw ones, and the raw ranges are the cautious ones.

We checked the headline numbers a second way. The NFL trend recomputed from the raw game file came out at −1.4 per decade again. The NBA trend recomputed from a separate box-score source came out at −2.6, against −2.7. And we re-read the 2020-21 empty-arena games straight from the raw files: 574 games, a 50.5% home win rate, matching game by game.

Denver lost at home to the Knicks 142 to 103 on March 6, 2026, a 39-point defeat in front of 19,807 people, the worst home loss of the 25 seasons. The building was full and the edge did nothing for them that night. An edge of two points a game is a tilt across a season. The data says that tilt is smaller than it was, larger in a few buildings than in most, and, by the best evidence we have, partly the people in the seats.

Data. NFL: nflverse game results with closing point spreads, 1999 to 2025 (2026 has only two played games and is left out). NBA: ESPN box scores and ESPN team schedules with per-game attendance, 2002 to 2026. Neutral-site games and the 88 bubble games are excluded, and one 0-0 record was dropped.

Method. Home edge is the home team’s win rate or average margin, with team quality accounted for where noted. Plausible ranges are 95% intervals: the span the true value would land in 95 times out of 100 if the study were repeated, found by redrawing whole home teams thousands of times. The empty-arena comparison sets each team against its own 2015 to 2019 home edge. The NBA 2020-21 and NFL 2020 seasons are left out of the trend lines and always shown separately.
