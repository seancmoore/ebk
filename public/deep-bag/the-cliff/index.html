The Cliff

Running backs go over it at 29. Receivers and NBA players get a year or two more, hockey skaters three to five. We rebuilt the aging curves for 14 position groups in four leagues, correcting for every player who quietly washed out of the data.

A Deep Bag investigation · 78,055 player-seasons · NFL, NBA, MLB, NHL
October 2026

In 2018, Todd Gurley was 24 years old and scoring 26.6 PPR fantasy points per game (fpts from here on), the kind of season that gets a running back paid. Two seasons later he was at 10.9. The season after that, at 27, he was out of the league. A degenerative knee did most of it, and that’s the point: however a player falls off, the chart just stops counting him.

Try to find Gurley in an “average production by age” chart. At 27 he had no stats, so as far as the chart knows, he never turned 27. The 27-year-old backs in that average are the ones who were still good enough to have a job. Meanwhile the far edge of the chart belongs to someone like Tom Brady, still scoring 22.0 fpts a game at 44, standing in for a whole draft class of quarterbacks who had been retired for a decade.

That’s survivorship bias, and it is baked into almost every aging chart you have seen. So we rebuilt the curves from scratch: every qualifying season in the NFL since 1999 (defense since 2013), the NBA since 2002, MLB since 1970 and the NHL since 2001. Here is when each position actually falls off, how sure we can be, and the season to sell your guy before he does.

01 The average says running backs peak at 30

Average every NFL running back’s fpts by age and the best age for a back comes out as 30. At 33 the average back is still at 78% of that peak. If that sounds wrong, count the players behind the average. The chart shows how many qualifying seasons (8 or more games) there are at each age.

There are 394 qualifying seasons at age 24. At 33 there are 24. The backs still carrying the ball at 30 are the ones who never slowed down, so the average at 30 is an average of exceptions.

Analysts caught this years ago. Their standard fix is the delta method: compare each player only with himself, season to season, and chain the year-over-year changes together. It sounds airtight. It isn’t quite. A player only gets a next season if a team kept him, and teams keep players partly on last year’s numbers, which include luck. So the delta method leans on players coming off lucky years, and when they fall back to earth, it books the fall as aging.

The other method, which we call the career model, grades every season against that player’s own career average, so one lucky year is one input among many. It has a blind spot of its own, explained in the next section. Here are all three answers for running backs, on a proportional scale (a back who loses 20% of his production is at 80%, whatever his level).

At 30, the two methods say the typical back has kept between 55% and 74% of his peak; by 33, between 25% and 51%. The naive average says he has lost nothing at all by 30.

02 How we know which line to trust

With real players there is no answer key, so we built one: thousands of fake careers with an aging curve we chose, and fake front offices that cut, bench and re-sign players the way real teams do. Then we asked each method to find the curve we had hidden. When every player ages on the same schedule, the career model nails it and the delta method runs about a year early. But real players don’t share a schedule. In our data, how fast players decline after 27 varies about as much as the decline itself: from 0.7 times the average for receivers to more than 3 times for quarterbacks. Build that in, and the career model can run up to a year slow, because the players still around at 33 are the ones aging slowest, and it reads their slope as everyone’s.

So neither method is the ruler. What held up is the pair: in every one of 11 simulated scenarios (12 leagues each), the true cliff age landed between the delta method and the career model. Think of them as two clocks, one known to run fast and one known to run slow. A later stress test with 14 more setups (decline rates that differ far more between players, noisier stats, flatter and steeper curves) found the same, with two limits: in leagues as noisy as a goalie’s or reliever’s season where everyone aged alike, the range missed about one league in four, and the trick works for an early line like ours, not for much deeper drops.

That is how we report it below: a range, with the early end from the delta method and the late end from the career model. When the two agree, you get one number.

Naive averages make athletes look ageless. The two honest methods disagree, and the truth lives in the gap.

03 Where the cliff actually is

What we mean by the cliff. Take a player who is better than 7 of every 10 established players at his position. The cliff is the age at which getting older, by itself, has cost the typical player enough to make that guy average. For a running back that is about 2.2 fpts a game, roughly the gap between the RB12 and the RB21 in points per game in a normal season. For a hitter it is about 9 points of OPS+, and for a hockey forward about 11 points over a full season. Because the yardstick is how far down his position’s pecking order a player slides, a goalie, a quarterback and a first baseman can be measured the same way. (For the stat people: half a standard deviation of true talent among players with two or more qualifying seasons. The report has the math.)

Here is every group on that yardstick, from the prime (green) through the slide (grey-green) to the cliff (red). The hatched red stretch is the range between the two methods.

Running backs are alone at the front: both methods put their cliff at 29, the only group where they agree on an age that early. Defensive backs follow at 30. Receivers, tight ends and linebackers go over at 30–31, defensive linemen at 31, and the NBA runs on the same clock as NFL receivers: 30–31. Quarterbacks and hockey skaters last longest: 32–33 for quarterbacks and forwards, 32–34 for defensemen. The quarterback number is the shakiest of those: with a 12-game bar instead of 8, or without the era adjustment, no quarterback cliff shows up by 33 or 34.

Is it really a cliff? For the typical player, no: it’s a hill that gets steeper. A running back loses about half a point a game between 26 and 27. Between 28 and 32 it is 0.7 to 1.1 points a year on the career model and 1.1 to 1.9 on the delta method. The cliffs you remember, Gurley’s and Bell’s, are that slope plus the injury or the benching that ends a career: the part an average-by-age chart never sees.

The wide ranges belong to baseball and goalies, and they are not all alike. A goalie’s or a reliever’s season is noisy: the luck in a single season is about 1.8 times (goalies) and 1.6 times (relievers) the spread in talent between players. In simulated leagues that noisy, the delta method ran 2.2 to 2.8 years early, while the career model stayed within about a year of the truth (slightly early when everyone aged alike, up to a year and a quarter late when they didn’t). So for those two the late end is the better bet, give or take a year: goalies 29 to 30, relievers 32 to 33. Starting pitchers (1.4) lean the same way, more weakly: 30 to 31. Hitters are different. A season of OPS+ is about as noisy as a quarterback’s (1.1), and in simulated leagues like that each method missed by about a year, in opposite directions. That leaves the middle of 30–34, 31 to 33, as the safest reading.

Baseball players do not get a pass, either. OPS+ makes hitters look ageless, because losing 18 points of an index looks like nothing on a chart, and it is a full standard deviation of hitting talent. On the talent yardstick, hitters age later than football players, but they age.

04 The fantasy answer: when to get off the bus

Every top fantasy finisher tends to give some back the next year, at every age, because part of last season was luck. So the useful question is how much more an older starter gives back. This is what actually happened to fantasy starters (top-24 running backs and receivers, top-12 quarterbacks and tight ends by season total) the following season, among those who played again. The advice is written for dynasty and keeper leagues, where you pick the moment to sell; in redraft, read “sell” as “let someone else draft him.”

Starters 24 and under give back between 0.6 and 1.5 fpts a game, depending on position. Running backs break from that pace at 27: starting backs aged 27 or 28 give back 3.2 fpts a game the next season, and past 30, 4.0. The cheat sheet strips out the part every starter loses, keeps only the extra drop that comes with age, and counts a season lost entirely as zero, because that is what you get from a player who doesn’t play.

Running backs. Pay full price through a back’s age-27 season: the career model still has the typical back in his prime at 27. Then sell. Starting backs aged 27 or 28 give back an extra 1.2 fpts a game the following season, and most of that belongs to the 28-year-olds: starters who were 27 gave back an extra 0.6, starters who were 28 an extra 2.2, and 29 is the cliff. So the winter before a back’s age-28 season is the safe sell, and the winter before his age-29 season is the last call. One caution for 2026: in the 2012–2025 half of the data alone the cliff comes at 28–29, against 30 in 1999–2011. Each half is thin at the older ages (the modern curve ends at 30), so treat it as a lean toward selling early, not a finding.

Stars are not exempt. They have more to lose: backs who were top-quarter producers at 23 to 25 lost 5.2 fpts a game between 26 and 30, and everyone else lost 1.8. As a share, the two groups end up closer (60% and 69% of what they had at 26). Age takes a cut, not a flat fee.

Receivers. A slower version of the same story. The extra drop is small at 27 or 28 (−0.5), grows at 29 or 30 (−1.2) and is −1.6 past 30, where a starter gives back about 3 fpts a game the following season. Sell before his age-30 season.

Tight ends. The cliff comes at 30–31, like receivers, but the tight ends still starting past 30 are a hardy group: the ones who played again held their production (+0.1 against young starters). Just 18 players account for those 44 seasons, led by Tony Gonzalez, Jason Witten and Shannon Sharpe. The risk is the zero: 14% of them played no games the next year, which is why the table reads −1.3. Four of those six had simply retired (Gonzalez, Sharpe, Witten, Gronkowski). In redraft you will know before the draft; in dynasty, that zero is the price of holding.

Quarterbacks. They do age: the cliff lands at 32–33, but the slope into it is gentle. Counting missed seasons, starters past 28 give back less than half a point a game more than young ones. Past 34 we don’t have enough of them to say.

The one-line version: sell running backs before their age-28 season (29 at the latest) and receivers before their age-30 season. A tight end still starting past 30 has already beaten the curve; the risk with him is retirement, not a slide. And don’t let a quarterback’s birthday scare you before 32.

The other way to lose is a player who doesn’t play at all. Among all backs with a qualifying season, the share with no games the next year rises from 16% at 26 to 25% at 30 (fantasy starters are safer: 6% of starting backs aged 29 or 30), and the chart shows tight ends climbing right with them.

Which brings us to this season. The box applies the rule to every 2025 fantasy starter who is in his sell window, near his position’s cliff or past it.

05 “But what about Derrick Henry?”

The cliff is an average, and the players we remember are the ones who beat it. Henry put up 19.8 fpts a game at 30 and 16.4 at 31. Frank Gore was still at 13.4 at 33. The chart puts three backs who beat the curve next to three who fell off it. Each back is drawn against the typical curve started from his own best season at 24 to 26.

Tony Gonzalez posted 13.7 at 37, Jerry Rice 15.9 at 39, and Peyton Manning had the best fantasy season of his career in our data at 37. LeBron James averaged a Game Score of 22.5 at 38. Barry Bonds posted an OPS+ of 264 at 39 (our version, without a park adjustment; Baseball-Reference lists 263), a season baseball still argues about.

The other list is longer and quieter. Le’Veon Bell went from 22.9 fpts a game at 25, sat out a year, and was at 6.8 by 28. Ezekiel Elliott went from 21.9 at 23 to 10.3 at 28. Shaun Alexander was at 23.6 at 28, above his own curve, and 9.5 two years later. Nobody makes a highlight reel of those seasons, which is exactly the problem: the survivors are loud, the departures are silent, and our intuition averages the loud ones.

06 What this can’t tell you

These are curves for the typical player, not predictions for your player. They measure production, which mixes skill with how much a coach chooses to use someone. That is the right thing for fantasy but not a pure measure of the body. The oldest ages rest on a handful of players. And the honest answer to “when” is a range, not a number: the two methods that survive our simulation disagree by up to 5 years for the noisiest stats, and no amount of data from the players who stayed can fully reveal how the ones who left would have aged.

The short version is still clear. Running backs fall first and fastest: sell them before their age-28 season. Receivers and NBA players get a year or two more. Hockey skaters get a few more than that. Hitters age later, not never. And Todd Gurley isn’t on the average-by-age chart. That’s the reason not to trust it.

Method. Aging curves on 78,055 qualifying player-seasons, estimated with the naive average by age, the standard delta method, and a player fixed-effects “career model” (every season compared with the same player’s career level), plus proportional (log) versions of the last two. Eight estimators were tested on simulated leagues with a known answer, in 11 scenarios, including ones where players age at different rates, plus a 14-case stress test. The cliff is the first age after the peak at which the typical player is half a talent-SD below it (talent-SD = spread of the persistent player effect among players with 2+ qualifying seasons, net of season-to-season noise), reported as the range between the delta method and the career model. Error bars in the charts are 95% intervals from resampling players 500 times; the cliff ranges are the gap between the two methods, which is usually wider. fpts = nflverse PPR points, 2-point conversions included. Age on Sept 1 (NFL), Feb 1 (NBA, NHL) or June 30 (MLB). Full math, sample counts, the simulation, robustness checks and a worked example are in the technical report (report_v04). Data: nflverse, hoopR/ESPN, Lahman Baseball Database, NHL stats API; 2026 roster status from Wikipedia and ESPN, October 4, 2026.
