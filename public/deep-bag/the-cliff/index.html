The Cliff

Running backs go over it at 29. Hockey skaters hang on until 32 or later. Both ages come from rebuilding the aging curves for 14 position groups in four leagues, this time counting the players who quietly vanished from the data.

Todd Gurley was 24 in 2018, and he averaged 26.6 PPR fantasy points a game for the Rams (fpts from here on). The year before, he had put up 25.6. That is the kind of run that gets a running back paid. In 2019 he fell to 14.6. In 2020 he was at 10.9. In 2021, the season he turned 27, he didn’t play at all.

A degenerative knee did most of that. How he fell off matters less here than what happened to him on paper afterward. Pull up the usual chart of production by age and look for Gurley. At 26 he is in it, dragging the average down. At 27 he is simply gone, and the chart never notices: the 27-year-old backs it averages are the ones who still had jobs. At the far edge of the same chart sits Tom Brady, still scoring 22.0 fpts a game at 44 and standing in for a whole draft class of quarterbacks who had been retired for a decade.

The name for this is survivorship bias, and the plain average-by-age chart is built on it. This study rebuilds the curves from 78,055 qualifying player-seasons: the NFL since 1999 (defense since 2013), the NBA since 2002, MLB since 1970 and the NHL since 2001. The aim is one age per position, the point where the typical player falls off, with the players who left counted.

A qualifying season

NFL: 8 or more games (defense: 8 or more with a defensive snap). NBA: 25 games. MLB: 200 plate appearances or 40 innings. NHL: 20 games for skaters, 15 for goalies. Thresholds shrink for shortened seasons.

The average back never ages

The chart most people would draw takes every NFL running back with at least eight games in a season, turns his fpts per game into a share of the best age, and averages by age. Its peak comes late. The bars underneath, the number of seasons behind each point, explain why.

By that chart the average back peaks at 30 and still has 78% of his peak at 33. There are 394 qualifying seasons at age 24 and only 24 at age 33, and the backs still carrying the ball at 30 are mostly the ones who never slowed down. The point at 30 describes a small group that aged unusually well.

Two clocks, one fast and one slow

Analysts spotted the problem years ago. The standard fix is the delta method: compare each player only with himself, season to season, and chain the year-over-year changes together. The catch is that a player only gets a next season if a team kept him, and teams keep players partly on last year’s numbers, luck included. So the delta method leans on players coming off lucky years, and when they fall back to earth it books the fall as aging.

The other approach, called the career model here, grades every season against that player’s own career level, so one lucky year is one input among many. It has its own blind spot, which comes up below. The next chart puts all three answers for running backs on a proportional scale, where a back who has lost a fifth of his production sits at 80%. The plain average runs along the top, and the two other lines split away beneath it after 26.

At 30 the two methods say the typical back has kept between 55% and 74% of his peak. By 33 it is between 25% and 51%. The plain average says he has lost nothing at all by 30.

Real players come with no answer key, so the study made one: simulated leagues with an aging curve chosen in advance, fake front offices that cut and bench players the way real teams do, and each method asked to find the hidden curve. When every player ages on the same schedule, the career model finds it exactly and the delta method runs about a year early. Real players don’t share a schedule. In the real data, the spread in how fast individual players decline in their late 20s and early 30s runs from 0.7 times the average decline for receivers to more than 3 times it for quarterbacks. Put that spread into the fake leagues and the career model starts running late, by up to a year, because the players still around at 33 are the slow agers and it reads their slope as everyone’s.

Used together, the two did better than either alone. The chart below shows how far each one missed the hidden cliff in 11 simulated worlds, 12 leagues apiece, with the true cliff as the dashed accent line at zero. The delta method’s markers land on it or to its left, the career model’s on it or to its right.

In every world the true cliff age fell inside the range the two methods set, sometimes right on one end of it: one clock known to run fast, one known to run slow. A stress test with 14 more setups (decline rates that differ far more between players, noisier stats, flatter and steeper curves) mostly agreed, with two exceptions. In leagues as noisy as a goalie’s or a reliever’s season where everyone aged alike, the range missed about one league in four. And the bracket holds for a cliff defined as early in the decline as this one; for much deeper drops it can fail.

From here on the cliff is a range, with the early end from the delta method and the late end from the career model. Where the two agree, it is a single age.

Where each sport falls off

The cliff needs a definition that works in every sport. Take a player who is better than 7 of every 10 established players at his position. The cliff is the age at which getting older, by itself, has cost the typical player enough to turn that guy into an average one. For a running back that is about 2.2 fpts a game, roughly the gap between the RB12 and the RB21 in a normal season. For a hitter it is about 9 points of OPS+, and for a hockey forward about 11 points over a full season. Because the yardstick is a slide down the pecking order, a goalie and a first baseman can be measured on it the same way as a running back.

For the stat people

The yardstick is half a standard deviation of true talent among players with two or more qualifying seasons, net of season-to-season noise. The technical report has the math and the robustness checks.

The chart below puts all 14 groups on that yardstick at once. Each ridge is one group’s career-model curve falling away from its peak, and the dashed line along its top is the delta method. The glass plane is the cliff line, half a talent-SD under the peak; where a ridge sinks through it is where that group goes over, and the accent strip on the glass is the range between the two methods. Running backs are the front ridge, and they go under years before the hockey skaters further back.

Running backs are alone at the front: both methods put their cliff at 29, the only group where they agree on an age that early. Defensive backs follow at 30. Receivers, tight ends and linebackers go over at 30 to 31, defensive linemen at 31, and NBA players run on the same clock as NFL receivers. Quarterbacks and hockey skaters last longest, 32 to 33 for quarterbacks and forwards and 32 to 34 for defensemen. The quarterback number is the shakiest of those. With a 12-game bar instead of 8, or without the era adjustment, no quarterback cliff shows up by 33 or 34.

Laid flat, the same results read as a timeline for each group: the prime, the slide after it, the cliff range in accent and whatever data runs past it. The noisy statistics sit at the bottom, and their ranges are visibly wider.

Up close, the typical decline is a slope that steepens. A running back loses about half a point a game between 26 and 27; between 28 and 32 he loses 0.7 to 1.1 points a year on the career model and 1.1 to 1.9 on the delta method. The sudden falls people remember, Gurley’s and Le’Veon Bell’s, are that slope plus an injury or a benching that ends the career, and an average-by-age chart drops those seasons along with the player.

The clip below turns the running back gap into fpts. By 30 the typical back is about 3 a game below his peak on the career model and 4.4 on the delta method, while the plain average shows no drop at all; receivers, tight ends and quarterbacks sit beside him for scale.

Why baseball and goalies get wide ranges

The wide ranges belong to baseball and to goalies, and they are not wide for the same reason. A goalie’s or a reliever’s season is noisy. Measured against the spread in talent between players, the luck in a single season is about 1.8 times as large for goalies and 1.6 times for relievers. The chart ranks all 14 groups by that ratio, with accent bars on the three where the noise changes which end of the range to trust.

In simulated leagues that noisy, the delta method ran 2.25 to 2.75 years early, while the career model stayed close: slightly early when everyone aged alike, and at most a year and a quarter late when they didn’t. For goalies and relievers the late end is the better bet, give or take a year, which puts goalies at 29 to 30 and relievers at 32 to 33. Starting pitchers (1.4) lean the same way, more weakly, at 30 to 31. Hitters are a different case. A season of OPS+ (1.1) is about as noisy as a quarterback’s (1.0), and in simulated leagues like that each method missed by about a year in opposite directions, which leaves the middle of their 30 to 34 range, 31 to 33, as the safest reading.

OPS+ can make hitters look ageless, because 18 points of an index centered on 100 barely registers on a chart. Those 18 points are a full standard deviation of hitting talent. On the talent yardstick hitters age later than football players, with the cliff somewhere in their early 30s.

When to sell

Every top fantasy finisher tends to give some of it back the next year, at every age, because part of last season was luck. Starters 24 and under give back between 0.6 and 1.5 fpts a game depending on position. What matters for a dynasty or keeper roster is how much more an older starter gives back. The sell rule uses fantasy starters (top-24 running backs and receivers, top-12 quarterbacks and tight ends by season total): their change the following season, minus what starters aged 23 to 26 at the same position lose, with a season missed entirely counted as zero, because that is what you get from a player who doesn’t play.

The grid below is that extra drop by position and age band, with running backs in the front row. Accent bars are drops of a full point or more, which is where the sell rule kicks in.

Running backs break first. Starting backs aged 27 or 28 give back an extra 1.2 fpts a game the next season, and most of it belongs to the 28-year-olds: starters who were 27 gave back an extra 0.6, starters who were 28 an extra 2.2. That makes the winter before a back’s age-28 season the safe time to sell and the winter before his age-29 season the last call. Receivers run about two years behind, with an extra 1.2 at 29 or 30 and 1.6 past 30. Quarterbacks barely move; counting missed seasons, starters past 28 give back less than half a point a game more than young ones.

Tight ends are the odd group. Their cliff comes at 30 to 31, like receivers, but the tight ends still starting past 30 are a hardy bunch, and the ones who played again held their production (+0.1 against young starters). Just 18 players account for those 44 seasons, led by Tony Gonzalez, Jason Witten and Shannon Sharpe. The risk with them is retirement: 14% played no games the next year, and four of the six who sat out had retired (Gonzalez, Sharpe, Witten and Rob Gronkowski).

Star backs age too. Backs who were top-quarter producers at 23 to 25 lost 5.2 fpts a game between 26 and 30, against 1.8 for everyone else. As a share the two groups end up closer, at 60% and 69% of what they had at 26: age takes a percentage from everyone, and the best backs have more to take.

The cheat sheet pulls the rules into one table. Accent cells are the drops that trigger a sale; the last column is the share of peak each position keeps at 30 across the two methods.

The other way to lose is a player who doesn’t play at all. Among all backs with a qualifying season, the share who played no games the next year rises from 16% at 26 to 25% by 29. Fantasy starters are safer, at 6% for starting backs aged 29 or 30. The lines below follow that share for the four fantasy positions, and after 28 the tight ends climb right alongside the backs.

The ones who beat it

The players everyone remembers are the ones who beat the average. Derrick Henry put up 19.8 fpts a game at 30 and 16.4 at 31. Frank Gore was still at 13.4 at 33. The panels below set three backs who beat the curve beside three who fell under it, each drawn against where the typical back would be if he started from that player’s best season at 24 to 26. Watch where each line leaves its band.

Outside the backfield the survivors are easier to find. Tony Gonzalez posted 13.7 fpts a game at 37, Jerry Rice 15.9 at 39, and Peyton Manning had the best fantasy season of his career in our data at 37. LeBron James averaged a Game Score of 22.5 at 38. Barry Bonds posted an OPS+ of 264 at 39 (our version, without a park adjustment; Baseball-Reference lists 263), a season baseball still argues about.

The other list is longer and quieter. Le’Veon Bell went from 22.9 fpts a game at 25 to 6.8 at 28, with a season he sat out in between. Ezekiel Elliott went from 21.9 at 23 to 10.3 at 28. Shaun Alexander was at 23.6 at 28, above his own curve, and 9.5 two years later. Nobody cuts a highlight reel of those seasons, which is how a gut sense of aging ends up built from Henry and Gore.

What the curves can’t see

Every curve here describes the typical player, and a particular player can sit well above or below it. The curves measure production, which mixes skill with how much a coach chooses to use someone. That suits fantasy and makes a poor reading of what the body itself is doing. The oldest ages rest on a handful of players in every sport.

Of all this, the order is the solid part: running backs go first and hockey skaters last under every check in the study. The exact age is a range, and for the noisiest statistics the two methods that survived the simulation sit up to 5 years apart. No amount of data from the players who stayed can fully reveal how the ones who left would have aged.

One question is still open, and it matters most to anyone holding a running back. Split the data in half and the backs of 2012 to 2025 go over the cliff at 28 to 29, against 30 for 1999 to 2011. Each half is thin at the older ages, and the modern curve stops at 30, so this is a lean toward selling earlier, short of a finding. If the cliff is moving younger, the backs who turn 28 over the next few seasons will show it. Jonathan Taylor, D’Andre Swift, Jaylen Warren and Kenneth Gainwell, all 27 this season, are first in line.

Method. Aging curves on 78,055 qualifying player-seasons, estimated with the plain average by age, the delta method and a player fixed-effects career model, plus proportional (log) versions of the last two. Eight estimators were tested on simulated leagues with a known answer in 11 scenarios, plus a 14-case stress test. The cliff is the first age after the peak at which the typical player is half a talent-SD below it, reported as the range between the delta method and the career model. Intervals come from resampling players 500 times. fpts are nflverse PPR points. Age on September 1 (NFL), February 1 (NBA, NHL) or June 30 (MLB).

Everything else (the full math, sample counts, the simulation, robustness checks and a worked example) is in the technical report.
