The Rubber Band Is Real. It Just Isn’t Magic.

Leaders in real NFL and NBA games give back more than team strength explains, and more than a simulated league that ignores the score does. In football it is the last five minutes. In basketball it never stops.

On Sunday, October 11, 2026, the Washington Commanders led the New York Giants 27 to 13 with 11:05 left in the fourth quarter. Jayden Daniels had just run 26 yards for a touchdown and added a two-point conversion. The pregame line had Washington by 3.5, and ESPN’s model gave it as much as 97.9% at its peak. In front of 64,195 people at Northwest Stadium, the game looked finished.

It was not. Jameis Winston hit Isaiah Likely for a 17-yard touchdown with 7:20 left. With 1:43 left he hit Malik Nabers for another 17, and the Giants kicked the extra point to make it 27 to 27. Overtime passed without a score, and the game went into the books as a tie.

Look at what each side did in between. From the moment it was 27 to 13, the Giants ran 19 plays from scrimmage and 95% of them were passes, one every 24 seconds. Washington ran 8 plays, 88% of them passes, one every 32 seconds. The team behind threw on nearly every snap. The team ahead did not run out the clock. The chart below follows Washington’s lead through the 60 minutes of regulation, and the thing to watch for is the last stretch, where it goes from 14 points to zero.

It is a good game to open with and weak evidence for anything. One game cannot tell a team that got hot from a team that simply had to throw. So this piece asks the question across 7,026 NFL games and 28,505 NBA games: do leads in real games shrink more than they should, and if they do, what is doing it?

What the rubber band is supposed to be

Fans call it the rubber band. A team builds a big lead, the lead stretches, and then it snaps back. The usual explanations are about people. The leader relaxes or plays not to lose. The trailer wakes up, the crowd comes alive, momentum swings. The comebacks are the games people remember.

A lead can shrink for four different reasons, and only the last one is the rubber band as fans mean it. The leader may be the weaker team, so the lead was luck and fades. Scoring comes in lumps, so a lead is often a burst that has just ended, and whatever follows looks like a comeback even when nothing has changed. Teams also change how they play when the score is lopsided: the leader runs the ball, the trailer throws it. And something extra could happen, a swing in effort or nerve that goes beyond the first three. All four produce shrinking leads, which is why the rubber band is hard to see.

Strength, at least, works against it. Leaders are usually the better team, so for the groups studied below they are expected to outscore the trailer by 1.06 points over the rest of an NFL game and 1.36 in the NBA.

A league where scoring ignores the score

To measure a rubber band you need a world with none. So we built one. We simulated 160,000 NFL games and 228,040 NBA games in which scoring never reacts to the score. There is no momentum, no clock management and no garbage time. Teams trade possessions, and each possession is drawn at random from real ones. The fake games are built to match real football and basketball in total scoring (43.97 points a game against 43.95 in the real NFL), in the size of the gaps between teams, and in pace.

The real games are every regular-season NFL game from 1999 to week 5 of 2026 (7,026 games) and every regular-season NBA game from 2003 to 2026 (28,505), regulation time only. At every 5-minute mark of an NFL game, and every 4-minute mark in the NBA, we found the team that was ahead and asked: from here to the end of regulation, who scores more, and by how much? Then we asked the same question of the fake games with the same lead, the same time left and the same pregame strength. Strength comes from the Vegas spread in the NFL and from a rating built from each team’s other games that season in the NBA.

Why not just use the odds?

Because even a world where nothing reacts to anything produces comebacks that look real. A run ends because the other side scored, so a team that just ripped off ten straight points looks, afterward, like it is being answered. The fake league contains all of that arithmetic and nothing else. Whatever real games do beyond it is the rubber band.

The gap between real and fake is what we call the rubber band: the points a lead gives back that strength and plain arithmetic do not explain. Baseball and hockey are missing. There is no play-by-play or scoring-by-period data for them on hand, and building it honestly, one request per game across tens of thousands of games, did not fit the study.

Leads do shrink

Start with NFL leads of 8 or more points, 28,477 snapshots from 5,808 games. In real games, the leader’s margin over the trailer across the rest of regulation averages −0.55 points (range −0.79 to −0.32), which means the trailer wins the remainder on average. In the fake games the same leaders gain 0.85, and strength alone says 1.06. The gap to the fake games is −1.52 points (range −1.76 to −1.31). The leader’s chance of winning sits 1.95 percentage points below the fake league’s, with a range of 1.4 to 2.6 points below.

The NBA shows the same thing at a larger scale. For leads of 15 or more (45,901 snapshots from 13,989 games) the leader’s margin over the trailer averages −2.08 over the rest of regulation, against 1.27 in the fake games and 1.36 from strength alone. The gap is −3.37 points (range −3.52 to −3.24).

A negative average does not mean most leaders blow it. In the NFL, 46.8% of leaders are outscored in the rest of regulation, against 42.1% in the fake games. In the NBA it is 52.6% against 44.6%, and 58.9% against 40.6% for leads of 15 or more. A few points on average, spread over a great many games. Fans remember the games where it was all of them.

Does a bigger lead stretch further? The chart below sorts leads by size and shows how many points each group gives back beyond the fake games. A longer bar to the left means more shrink.

It does. NFL leads of 1 to 3 points give back 0.39, leads of 8 to 10 give back 1.19, leads of 15 to 21 give back 1.69, and leads of 22 or more give back 1.82 (range −2.17 to −1.48). In the NBA it climbs from 0.69 for leads of 1 to 4 to 2.60 for 10 to 14 and 3.62 for 20 or more (range −3.78 to −3.41). It is not fading, either: across three eras the shrink for all leads runs −0.83, −1.06 and −1.16 in the NFL, and −1.77, −1.85 and −1.81 in the NBA.

Football: the last five minutes

In football the shrink has an address. Take the 3,175 games in which a team led by 8 or more at halftime. By the end of regulation those leaders had given back 1.32 points beyond the fake games (range −1.62 to −1.02). The chart below splits that into the stretches of the second half. The left panel is the NFL; the right panel is the NBA, which gets its own section next.

The third quarter gives back 0.11 points, a number the data cannot tell from zero (range −0.12 to 0.32). The rest of the fourth quarter gives back 0.27. The last five minutes give back 1.16 (range −1.32 to −1.00), which is 88% of the total.

Something is going on in those five minutes, so we looked at every play in the last five minutes of every NFL game in the data and sorted them by the score. The chart below shows how often each kind of team passes, and how long it takes between snaps.

A team up 8 to 14 points throws on 19% of its plays and takes 22 seconds between snaps. A team down 8 to 14 throws on 93% of its plays, goes no-huddle on 20% of them and takes 14 seconds. The gap is 10.4 seconds a snap (range 9.9 to 10.8), across 8,256 plays for the leaders and 16,794 for the trailers. Early in the game nobody behaves like this: in the first half, teams up and down 8 to 14 throw on 61% and 66% of their plays, and both take about 26 seconds.

Then there is the kneel. 49% of drives that begin in the last five minutes with a lead of 8 or more include a kneel-down, against 18% when the game is tied or within 3 points. Leaders’ drives in that stretch are worth 0.43 points each, compared with 2.02 for leaders earlier in the game. Trailers’ drives are worth 1.45.

We can split the shrink exactly into who did what. For leads of 8 or more, the total across the drives that follow each mark is −1.46 points (range −1.65 to −1.27). Of that, 1.07 points come from the leader’s drives being worth less than in the fake games and 0.54 from the trailer’s drives being worth more. The number of drives hardly matters: it adds 0.15 points in the leader’s favour. So the clock gets into the game through what each possession is worth, not through who gets more of them.

The Giants game, one more time

Treat the opening game as an illustration of what trailing teams do in the last 11 minutes, and nothing more. It is not evidence of momentum. We could not verify who called what, or anything about injuries or why. And Washington, the leader, did not play the leader’s usual game: 8 plays, 88% of them passes.

The shrink is also bigger once a game is decided: −1.73 in garbage time against −0.76 in stretches where the fake league still gave the game a real chance. That fits teams managing a lead rather than feeling one.

Strategy is what we can see. Whether some of the late shrink is also the trailer playing with more urgency is not something this data can separate. And the numbers are averages over leads of 8 points or more; individual games vary hugely.

Basketball: the leak that never stops

The NBA has no such address. Teams up 15 or more at halftime (4,222 games) gave back 4.51 points beyond the fake games (range −4.80 to −4.17): 1.70 in the third quarter, 1.32 in the fourth until the last 4 minutes, and 1.49 in the last 4 minutes. That is the right panel of the chart above. The first half leaks as well. Teams up 15 or more at the end of the first quarter (791 games) gave back 2.10 points in the second quarter alone (range −2.70 to −1.50).

Another view needs no lead bins at all. Take any quarter, work out how much a team won or lost it by beyond what its strength predicts, and see what happens in the next one. The chart below shows that link in real games and in the fake ones, with zero meaning no link.

In the NBA, winning a quarter predicts losing ground in the next one at every step: a link of −0.153 from the first quarter to the second (range −0.167 to −0.139), −0.120 from the second to the third, −0.150 from the third to the fourth, and −0.270 from the first half to the second half (range −0.282 to −0.258). The fake games give about −0.010. The NFL link is close to nothing early (−0.029 from the first quarter to the second, 0.002 from the second to the third) and shows up late, at −0.139 from the third to the fourth, against −0.030 in the fake games.

The NBA shrink is there in competitive stretches (−1.52, range −1.59 to −1.46, from 205,678 snapshots) and in garbage time (−2.63). With ESPN’s own pregame spread as the strength yardstick (2020 to 2026) the gap for leads of 15 or more is −3.45 (range −3.74 to −3.19).

Some of the late leak is plain strategy. In the last 2 minutes of games within 10 points, the trailing team commits 2.22 times as many fouls as the leader (18,210 games), and in the last 4 minutes trailing teams take 34.4% of their shots from three against 31.4% for leaders. The first half has no such story we can see. The foul ratio there is 0.91, and teams up 15 or more and down 15 or more score about the same per 100 possessions, 116 and 115. So the leak is there from the start and we cannot point to what causes it. Rotations, substitution patterns and coaching adjustments are plausible causes we cannot observe, because the data has no lineups. Momentum and complacency cannot be separated from them either.

It takes two teams

One more test asks whether the shrink needs the opponent at all. We kept each team’s real scoring timeline and paired it with an opposing timeline from a different game, so neither team could react to the other. The chart below compares three worlds for each league: the fake games, real scoring with mismatched opponents, and real games.

With mismatched opponents, most of the shrink goes away. In the NFL, leads of 8 or more give back 0.53 points (range −0.64 to −0.42), against 1.52 in real games. In the NBA, leads of 15 or more give back 0.61 (range −0.71 to −0.53), against 3.37. The rubber band, such as it is, mostly exists when the two teams are actually playing each other. One caution: for the very biggest NFL leads, 22 or more, the mismatched version still shows −1.37, so part of the largest NFL shrinks comes from how one team plays when it is far ahead, not from the opponent.

Do they answer every run?

The announcer’s version of the rubber band is the run. One team goes on a 12-0 run, the other team answers, and everyone nods. We counted runs in real and fake games, where a run is consecutive scoring by one side while the other scores nothing. Then we measured what happened to the run team in the next 3 minutes in the NBA and the next 10 in the NFL. The chart below compares real and fake games after taking out what team strength predicts.

The trap is that a fake league with no reaction at all “answers” a lot of runs, because a run ends when the other side scores. In the NBA, after runs of 10 or more points, the other side outscored the run team over the next 3 minutes in 57% of fake cases and 62% of real ones (31,244 real runs). Beyond strength, the run team slips 1.81 points in real games and 1.33 in the fake ones, a difference of −0.48 (range −0.55 to −0.41). For runs of 15 or more the difference is −0.12 (range −0.41 to 0.17), no different from zero. In the NFL, after runs of 14 or more unanswered points (4,583 runs), the difference over the next 10 minutes is −0.27 (range −0.41 to −0.14), and the other side outscored the run team 63% of the time in real games and 63% in fake ones.

So “they always answer a run” is mostly arithmetic. Real games add a small push, clear for the shorter NBA runs and for the NFL, not for the longest NBA runs. Real NBA games also have 110 runs of 10 points per 100 games where the fake league has 35, because real basketball sometimes gives one team several possessions in a row and the fake league alternates strictly. So we read nothing into how often runs happen, and the after-run comparison is a bit generous to the fake league’s arithmetic.

What a fan can take from it

The general force, the team ahead going flat and the team behind getting hot, is not what the data shows. The cross-game test says the shrink needs the actual opponent, the run test says runs are answered only slightly more than a no-reaction world would answer them, and the NFL pattern lines up with play-calling, not feeling.

That does not make a lead safe. In the NFL a leader’s chance of winning sits 1.95 percentage points below what a no-reaction world gives, on average, and some leads that world calls all but certain were lost anyway. As for betting and fantasy, the study does not say. Our NFL strength yardstick is the closing Vegas spread, but we did not test whether the spread, in-game odds or any market already prices in the late shrink, and we have no NBA betting lines at all. We make no claim that any of this is an edge, and we would not bet on one on the strength of these numbers.

What we cannot tell you

The fake league is a model, and it is not a perfect twin of the real games. It matches total scoring, the size of the gaps between teams and pace, but its margins are more spread out than real ones around the prediction: 14.27 points against 13.16 in the NFL, and 13.07 against 12.38 in the NBA. That gap is itself part of the effect, since real games are more tightly bunched, but it means the size of the headline numbers depends on the fake league. The cross-game test and the quarter-to-quarter links do not use it, and they point the same way.

The strength yardsticks are noisy. The NBA rating uses games played after the one being predicted, and hidden strength that shows up during a game, an injury or a rest day, would push our estimates toward zero, not away from it. The ranges treat games as independent although snapshots within a game overlap. Many comparisons were run, so the headline gaps deserve more weight than small subgroup differences such as home against road, which should not be quoted.

The data has holes. In the NFL, 16 games were dropped for impossible scores, and 2026 is only 78 games through week 5. In the NBA, the ESPN feed misses whole games in some seasons, so 2002 was dropped and 2003 is partial. There are no playoffs, no overtime points, and no MLB or NHL.

And the biggest hole is a cause. In the NFL the timing and the play-calling match, and we say so. In the NBA, nothing we can observe explains the leak in the first half. Nothing here was an experiment, and none could have been, so we cannot say what would happen if a team simply played differently with a lead.

Washington never trailed in regulation, and it still gave back a 14-point lead in the last 11 minutes. By the numbers here, the band is real: 1.52 points in the NFL, most of it in the last five minutes, and 3.37 in the NBA, spread across the whole game. In football it lines up with play-calling. In basketball it lines up with something we cannot yet see. Neither needs magic.

The Giants and Commanders facts come from ESPN’s public game summary, cross-checked with nflverse. Regulation time only.
