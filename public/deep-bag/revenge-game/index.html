Payback Is a Rounding Error

Across 1,547 NFL games against a former team, the revenge bump comes to about a third of a fantasy point. Home field is worth about the same.

Tyreek Hill’s first game against Kansas City was played in Frankfurt, in Week 9 of 2023, after six seasons as a Chief. He caught 8 passes for 62 yards, lost a fumble that the Chiefs returned for a touchdown, and Miami lost 21-14. Based on the rest of his season and the matchup, our model expected 23.6 fantasy points from him (PPR scoring; fpts from here on). He scored 12.5.

Nine years earlier Steve Smith Sr. did the opposite. In Week 4 of 2014, in his first game against the Carolina team that had let him go after 13 seasons, he caught 7 passes for 139 yards and 2 touchdowns in a 38-10 Baltimore win. That was 32.9 fpts against an expected 12.2. Smith’s afternoon gets replayed every time a player’s old team comes up on the schedule. Hill’s has mostly been forgotten.

What counts

Any regular-season game in which a quarterback, running back, receiver or tight end faces a franchise he played at least 8 games for, about half a season. Relocated teams count as one franchise, so Oakland and Las Vegas are both the Raiders. Careers start in 1999 or later, so every player’s full history is in the data and the first revenge games arrive in 2001.

Counted properly, there have been 1,547 NFL revenge games since 2001, played by 620 players. Against a former team, a player scores about a third of a fantasy point more than in an ordinary game: +0.35 fpts, with a 95% interval from +0.03 to +0.68. The edge survives most of the attempts to break it, and it is small enough that you should almost never act on it.

A third of a point

The hard part is the comparison. “He scored 20 against his old team” means nothing if he scores 20 every week, so each revenge game is measured against the same player’s other games that season for the same team. The model also adjusts for four things that move every fantasy score: how generous the defense had been to his position, home or away, the Vegas total for his team, and whether it was a division game. What remains is the question people argue about on Sunday mornings. Did he do more than he would have in an ordinary game with the same setup?

A third of a point is under 5% of a typical game in the sample, which averages 7.7 fpts once backups are counted. The chart below sets it beside the things fantasy players already weigh every week, plus one row they rarely look at: how far a player’s score swings from one ordinary game to the next. That bottom row is the scale for everything above it.

Revenge lands between home field (+0.31) and a defense one notch softer than average against his position (+0.39). The gap between one of the league’s softest defenses and one of its toughest, +1.20, is more than three times as big, and the ordinary week-to-week swing of 5.9 fpts is about 17 times the revenge bump.

The estimate went through 14 variations: dropping every control, adding the playoffs, requiring a longer stay with the old team, filling in the games where a player was on the field and never touched the ball, letting each player’s season drift on its own trend. It moved between +0.18 and +0.72, and 12 of the 14 versions kept their intervals above zero. No single player carries it. Ryan Fitzpatrick has the most revenge games in the data, 22 of them against five old employers, and taking him out leaves +0.30.

Seventy-seven star returns

Before building the model we wrote down 24 famous revenge games, and the list was not innocent. Jarvis Landry’s 36.8 fpts against Miami in 2019 and Jerry Jeudy’s 40.5 against Denver in 2024 made it because they turned up near the top of our own tables, which is how memory builds its list, too. Fifteen of the 24 beat the model’s expectation.

So the star return got a rule instead: the first meeting after a player left a team he had played at least 32 games for and averaged 14 or more fpts with. There are 77 of them, from 2005 to 2025. The chart compares them with the hand-picked list, with all 1,547 revenge games and with every ordinary game in the sample. The first two rows of each panel are the comparison that matters.

The hand-picked list beat expectation 62.5% of the time, by 3.8 fpts on average. The 77 star returns beat it 41.6% of the time, about as often as an ordinary week does (41.8%), and by less than a tenth of a point on average.

Chosen by memory, revenge games look like a law of nature. Chosen by rule, they look like any other Sunday.

The twelve biggest swings among those 77 split six over expectation and six under, and the names in the bottom half are as famous as the ones on top.

Hill, Emmanuel Sanders, Greg Jennings, Deshaun Watson, Tom Brady and Wes Welker are hardly obscure. Brady’s return to Foxborough in 2021, after 20 seasons as a Patriot, is remembered for the career passing-yards record he broke that night; he finished with 11.1 fpts, about 10 under expectation. Watson’s return to Houston in 2022 was his first start after his suspension, and he managed 5.3. Hill’s night in Frankfurt sits at the very bottom, the furthest short of all 77. Of the 77, 45 fell short of expectation, and the median came in 1.2 fpts under. The good end of the board is longer, with Landry, Smith and Andre Johnson all beating expectation by more than 20, and that is the end that gets the airtime.

The 77 can’t settle whether stars get a bump of their own. Their average is +0.06, and the interval runs from −1.6 to +1.6, wide enough to include the +0.35 from the full sample.

Grudges that can’t exist

If a third of a point is about revenge, it should vanish where revenge is impossible. There are two ways to fake it. One uses games against a team the player had not joined yet but later would: the same kind of tie to a franchise, with no grudge attached. The other hands every player a random “former team” he never played for and reruns the model, 1,000 times over.

The random former teams behave. They center on zero (−0.01), and only 2.9% of them produce a result as strong as the real one when measured on the same footing. Pooled, the future-team version is flat too: +0.02 across 2,921 games, against +0.35 for the real thing.

Split by timing, it stops looking flat. That cut was made after seeing the data, so it counts as a lead and nothing firmer. In the grid below each row pairs the real thing (teams a player had left) with the fake (teams he would join later). The front row holds every game, and the two rows behind it split them by how close the game came to the move. The back pair, games within a season of the move, is where the fake catches up.

Within a season of the move, the fake and the real are the same size: +0.43 against teams a player was about to join, +0.41 against teams he had just left. Two or more seasons away, the fake falls to −0.08 while the real one holds at +0.32. All four of those intervals cross zero.

The shape is still informative. Teams chasing players who just played well against them would produce a bump before the move, and that would not touch revenge games, which come after it. A player who simply reads one franchise’s defense well would produce both, before he joins it and after he leaves, and he is exactly the kind of player a team goes out and signs.

Two blind spots

Only players good enough to land a second job get revenge games, and a player who is active on revenge day may be in slightly better form than usual, which would push the number up. And the old team knows his habits while he knows their playbook, so the estimate is the net of both.

The bump is probably real and certainly small. Whether it has anything to do with revenge, this data can’t say.

Nobody in particular

The average could be hiding a group where the effect is big: players who were traded away, say, or the first meeting, when the cameras find him in warmups. The data was split 28 ways, and the chart shows the splits people argue about most. A group that carried the whole effect would show up as a bar well past the full-sample +0.35.

The first meeting, the one with the pregame montage, came in at +0.20, smaller than later meetings. Players who were traded away ran hotter at +0.69, with an interval that includes zero. Quarterbacks came in at +0.20, with an interval running from −1.29 to +1.55.

The split that stands out is how good a player was coming in. Players who averaged 8 to 14 fpts the season before got +0.88, and players under 8 got +0.01. It is the biggest gap on the chart and still one of 28 slices: after correcting for that many tries it doesn’t clear the bar, and its shape changes when “level” is measured another way. It stays a lead.

Is it a small lift for everyone or a handful of monster games? Drop the 10 biggest revenge games and the estimate falls to +0.20. Ordinary games given an identical lift lose the same amount when their 10 biggest are dropped, because a few huge afternoons sit on top of any pile of fantasy scores, so this test can’t tell the two stories apart. If anything, revenge games had fewer duds. Just 1.9% came in 10 or more fpts under a player’s average, against 3.3% of other games, a borderline difference. The extra shows up as yardage, about 2 more receiving yards for receivers and 2 more rushing yards for backs, with touchdowns flat, and none of those is clearly different from zero on its own.

Basketball fails the placebo

Basketball offers far more games: 13,559 against a former team (20 or more games there) from 2002-03 through 2022-23. LeBron James went back to Cleveland on December 2, 2010, and scored 38, about 11 more than expected. Kevin Durant scored 39 in his first game against Oklahoma City, in November 2016.

On average NBA players score 0.26 more points than expected against a former team, and with this much data the interval is tight, +0.16 to +0.37. The trouble is that they also score more against teams they will join later. The grid below is laid out like the NFL one, real on the left and fake on the right, with the season of the move at the back and two or more seasons away in front. The two columns step down together.

Against a team he left, a player scores +0.68 in the same season as the move and +0.18 two or more seasons later. Against a team he will join, it is +0.40 and then +0.12. A grudge has no reason to fade on the same schedule as a team’s interest in a player it hasn’t signed yet.

Subtract the placebo and +0.12 points a game is left, with an interval that starts right at zero. The cleanest test, each player against the same franchise before he joined and after he left, gives −0.03. That test has a blind spot of its own: it needs games from before a player joined a team, so it never sees a return to his first one. Those returns are 45% of NBA revenge games, and they run hotter, +0.35 against +0.18 for later teams.

One split does separate from the placebo, and it is the split a grudge would predict. Players score +0.38 walking into the old arena and +0.13 hosting their old team, while games against future teams come out the same at either end (+0.14 on the road, +0.13 at home). It is the most revenge-like result in either league, and it still falls short of the usual bar once the other splits are counted.

On Sunday

Treat a revenge game the way you treat home field, as a tiebreaker between two players you already can’t separate. Don’t bench the better player to chase a storyline, and in DFS nothing here says to pay up for one.

Two refinements, for anyone who sets lineups by formula. Using only what you knew at kickoff, and comparing with other players who had changed teams, the bump is about +0.39. Against all players it shrinks to +0.18, because players on new teams tend to fade over a season compared with everyone else, so their early weeks set a bar their later games miss. For real starters, 10 or more fpts a game the season before, it is +0.72, and that slice isn’t reliably different from everyone else (p = 0.14 for the gap).

Even the starters’ +0.72 is smaller than the 1.20 that separates a soft matchup from a tough one. When a receiver faces his old team, give him the third of a point you would give him for playing at home, and look at the defense first.

Data. nflverse weekly player stats, schedules, Vegas lines, player IDs and snap counts; the nflverse/nfldata trade ledger from Lee Sharpe; hoopR/ESPN NBA box scores (our NBA archive ends with 2022-23, and that season is missing its last few games per team).

Method. Player-season-team fixed effects with controls for opponent defense against the position, home, Vegas implied points and division games; intervals from a player-clustered bootstrap; future-team and fake-team placebos; 28 NFL subgroups with a Benjamini-Hochberg correction. fpts are PPR fantasy points.
