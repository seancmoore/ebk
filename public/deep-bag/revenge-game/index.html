Payback Is a
Rounding Error

Every revenge game gets its own pregame segment. We scored 1,547 of them. The payback is about a third of a fantasy point, about the same as playing at home.

One of these you remember.

In Week 4 of 2014, Steve Smith Sr. played the Carolina Panthers for the first time since they let him go. He had played 191 games for them, playoffs included. That afternoon he caught 7 passes for 139 yards and 2 touchdowns, Baltimore won 38–10, and he scored 32.9 fantasy points (PPR scoring; fpts from here on). Based on the rest of his season and that matchup, our model expected 12.2. Of the 77 star returns we track below, only one beat expectation by more.

Seven years later Tom Brady went back to Foxborough after 326 games as a Patriot. That was the night he broke Drew Brees's career passing-yards record, and Tampa Bay won 19–17. He finished with 11.1 fpts; we'd have expected 21.2. Tyreek Hill's first shot at Kansas City came in Frankfurt: 8 catches for 62 yards and a fumble the Chiefs returned for a touchdown. That's 12.5 fpts against an expectation of 23.6.

Nobody remembers Brady's box score from that night, or Hill's from Frankfurt. Everyone remembers Smith's. So we counted them. All 1,547.

01 How we scored it

A revenge game, for us, is any game where a quarterback, running back, receiver or tight end faces a franchise he played at least 8 games for, roughly half a season. Relocated teams count as the same franchise, so Oakland and Las Vegas are one team. We used every player whose career started in 1999 or later, so we could see his whole history. That leaves out Brett Favre's Vikings years, though adding the older players back barely changes the answer. The count: 1,547 revenge games by 620 players.

The hard part is the baseline. "He scored 20 against his old team" means nothing if he scores 20 every week. So every revenge game is compared with the same player's other games that season, for the same team. Then we adjust for four things: how generous the defense was to his position, whether he was at home, what Vegas expected his team to score, and whether it was a division game. What's left is the question everyone argues about: did he do more than he would have in a normal game?

02 The answer: about a third of a point

Players score +0.35 fpts more against a former team than in an ordinary game with the same setup. That's under 5% of a typical game in our sample (7.7 fpts; the sample includes backups). The 95% range runs from +0.03 to +0.68.

To see how small that is, set it next to the things fantasy players already weigh every week, plus one bar you don't usually see: how much a player's score bounces around from one normal game to the next.

Revenge is about the size of home field (+0.31) or a somewhat softer defense (+0.39). The gap between facing one of the league's softest defenses and one of its toughest is more than three times as big (+1.20). The ordinary week-to-week swing in his output (5.9 fpts) is 17 times as big. If you've ever watched a receiver carry your team one Sunday and vanish the next, you've seen swings that dwarf anything revenge adds.

We tried 14 ways to break it. Strip every control, add the playoffs, count only long tenures, fill in the games where a guy played and never touched the ball, let every player's season drift up or down on its own: the number lands between +0.18 and +0.72, and 12 of the 14 versions clear zero.

03 The games you remember are the wrong sample

When we wrote down 24 famous revenge games, 15 of them beat expectation. That sounds like proof, until you look at how we built the list. Several entries, like Jarvis Landry's 36.8 against Miami and Jerry Jeudy's 40.5 against Denver, got on it because we spotted them among the biggest games in our own data. That's how memory builds its list too.

So we defined the headline revenge game objectively instead: the first meeting after a player left a team he'd played at least 32 games for and averaged 14+ fpts with. A star going back. There are 77 of those, from 2005 to 2025. Here they are next to our famous list and next to all 1,547 revenge games.

A normal game beats expectation only about four times in 10, because a few huge weeks drag every player's average up. The famous list beat it six times in 10. The stars' actual first games back: four in 10, right at normal. Every dot below is one of those 77.

The right tail is real and it's spectacular, which is exactly why it's what you remember. But most of the games sit on the left: 58% fell short, and the median return came in 1.2 fpts under. The left tail is shorter, but look who's in it. The six worst star returns in our data belong to Tyreek Hill, Emmanuel Sanders, Greg Jennings, Deshaun Watson, Tom Brady and Wes Welker.

One caution: 77 games can't tell +0.06 from +0.35 (the range on that average runs from −1.6 to +1.6). This isn't proof that stars get nothing. It's proof that the stories we tell aren't a fair sample.

For every Steve Smith, there's a Tyreek Hill. We only tell one of those stories.

04 Is it real?

If a third of a point is real, it should disappear where revenge is impossible. So we ran two fake versions. First, games against a team the player hadn't joined yet but later would: same kind of connection, no grudge. Second, we gave every player a random "former team" he never played for and re-ran everything, 1,000 times.

Here's the real number next to both fakes.

The fake former teams behave: they center on zero, and only 2.9% of them produced a result as strong as the real one. The future-team placebo is flat overall (+0.02). But split it by timing, a cut we made after seeing the data, and a bump appears in the season right before a player joins a team: +0.46, about the size of the revenge effect itself, on 547 games and with a range that includes zero.

Teams chasing players who just played well against them would explain the placebo, but not revenge games, which come after the move. A lasting edge could explain both: a player who reads one franchise's defense well before he joins it and after he leaves, exactly the kind of player teams would want.

So here's where we land. It's probably not zero. It's definitely small. And we can't prove it's about revenge.

Two blind spots

Only players good enough to land a second job get revenge games, and one who is active on revenge day may be in slightly better form than usual, which would inflate our number. And the old team knows his habits while he knows their playbook, so we measure the net of both.

05 Who gets it? We can't tell

Maybe the average hides a group where it's big: players who were traded away, or the very first meeting, when the emotion is highest. We split the data 28 ways and plotted the ones people argue about most, with the overall +0.35 as a dotted line.

The first meeting, the one with the pregame montage, came in at +0.20, no bigger than later meetings. Traded players looked a bit hotter (+0.69), with a range that includes zero. Quarterbacks: +0.20, with a range from −1.3 to +1.5. We can't tell.

The split that stands out is how good a player was coming in. Players who averaged 8 to 14 fpts the season before got +0.88; depth players got about nothing (+0.01). It's the biggest gap on the chart, but it's one of 28 slices, and after correcting for testing that many it doesn't clear the bar.

Is it a small lift for everyone or a few monster games? Drop the 10 biggest revenge games and the effect halves, to +0.20, but that happens to any 1,547 fantasy games, because a few huge afternoons always sit on top. So that test settles nothing. If anything, revenge games had fewer duds (1.9% came in 10+ fpts under a player's average, versus 3.3% of other games), though that edge is borderline.

If it's anything, it's yards

Receivers averaged about 2 extra receiving yards and running backs about 2 extra rushing yards; touchdowns didn't move. None of it is clearly different from zero on its own.

06 The NBA version: same bump, bigger catch

Basketball gives us far more data: 13,559 games against a former team (20+ games there) from 2002–03 through 2022–23. LeBron James went back to Cleveland in December 2010 and scored 38, about 11 over expectation. Kevin Durant scored 39 in his first game against Oklahoma City.

On average, NBA players score +0.26 more points against a former team, and with this much data the range is tight.

But the NBA fails the placebo test. Players also score +0.14 more against teams they will join later, and that bump is biggest right around the move and fades with distance, just like revenge. The cleanest test compares each player with himself against the same franchise, before he joined and after he left. The four numbers side by side:

Subtracting the placebo leaves +0.12 points a game, about an eighth of a free throw, with a range that starts right at zero. The before-and-after comparison gives −0.03, nothing, but it only works for players who'd faced a team before joining it. It can't see returns to a player's first team, 45% of NBA revenge games, and those run hotter (+0.35, versus +0.18 against later teams).

One split stands out: +0.38 points walking into the old arena, +0.13 hosting the old team, a gap the placebo doesn't show (+0.13 and +0.14). That's the most revenge-like signal in the study, though it's one of five splits we tried and doesn't clear the bar once you account for that.

07 So, should you start him?

Treat the revenge game the way you treat home field: a tiebreaker between two players you already can't separate, and nothing more. Never bench a better player to chase a storyline, and in DFS, nothing here says to pay up for one.

Two refinements. Using only what you knew at kickoff, and comparing with other players who had changed teams, the bump is about +0.39. (Against all players it's +0.18, because players on new teams tend to fade over a season compared with everyone else.) For real starters, 10+ fpts a game last season, it's +0.72, but that's the kind of slice we warned about above: not reliably different from other players, and still smaller than the gap between a soft matchup and a tough one.

If a receiver is on your bench because he isn't good enough to start, a grudge won't change that.

Revenge is worth about as much as home field. You don't start players for home field.
§

Steve Smith's afternoon against Carolina really happened, and so did Brady's quiet night in Foxborough. Run all 1,547 and the Smiths and the Bradys nearly cancel, leaving a third of a point. We remember the Smiths because they make the better story.

How we did it. Data: nflverse weekly player stats, schedules and Vegas lines, player IDs and snap counts; nflverse/nfldata trade ledger; hoopR/ESPN NBA box scores (our NBA archive ends with 2022–23, and that season is missing its last few games per team). Method: player-season-team fixed effects with controls for opponent defense vs position, home, Vegas implied points and division; player-clustered bootstrap intervals; future-team and fake-team placebos; 28 subgroups with multiple-testing correction. fpts = PPR fantasy points.
